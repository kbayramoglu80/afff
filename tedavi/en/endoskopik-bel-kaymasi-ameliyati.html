Spondylolisthesis and Endoscopic Surgery

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is spondylolisthesis, and can it be treated endoscopically? Spondylolisthesis is forward slippage of one vertebra over the one below it. It may cause back or leg pain and sometimes nerve compression. Most patients are initially treated without surgery. If surgery is needed, the aim is to relieve nerve compression and stabilise the segment when indicated. Endoscopic techniques are an option only for selected cases.

Causes and types

TypeDescription
DegenerativeRelated to age-associated changes in the discs and facet joints; most common at L4–L5 and in older adults
IsthmicDue to a fracture or stress defect in the pars interarticularis; may occur in younger people and athletes
OtherCongenital, trauma-related, or postoperative slippage

Symptoms

  • Back pain that changes when bending forward or standing for long periods
  • Pain radiating into the leg, numbness, or pain that worsens while walking
  • Weakness in the leg or foot
  • A step-like feeling in the lower back or postural changes in some patients
  • Some cases cause no symptoms

Emergency: Seek emergency care without delay for weakness or numbness in both legs, loss of sensation around the groin or anus, or loss of bladder or bowel control or inability to pass urine (cauda equina syndrome).

How is it diagnosed?

A standing lateral X-ray shows the degree of slippage; flexion-extension X-rays assess instability. MRI shows nerve compression, while CT provides detail about bone structure and a pars defect. Treatment decisions depend on the degree of slippage, instability, and nerve compression.

Treatment options

ConditionManagement
Mild slippage with no or mild symptomsObservation, exercise, core strengthening, weight management, and medication; activity modification for athletes
Nerve compression with stable, low-grade slippageEndoscopic decompression may be considered for selected patients who do not improve with non-surgical treatment
Instability, progressive slippage, or high-grade slippageStabilisation of the affected segment with fusion (screws and a cage), using a minimally invasive or open technique
Pars defect in a young patientPars repair or fusion in selected patients

Decompression alone can make a slipped segment less stable. When instability is present, nerve decompression alone may not be sufficient and fusion may be needed.

The role of endoscopic surgery

  • With an endoscopic technique, a camera and small incision are used to remove ligament and bone tissue compressing the nerves
  • This approach may be considered for low-grade slippage without instability
  • Fusion cases require appropriate equipment and experience and are not performed at every centre
  • The clinician determines the appropriate method after examination and imaging

Recovery

  • After decompression, most patients are up within days; fusion usually involves a longer hospital stay and recovery period
  • Bone healing after fusion takes months; heavy lifting and strenuous movement are restricted during this time
  • Physiotherapy and core-strengthening exercises are planned

Potential risks

  • Dural tear or nerve injury (rare)
  • Infection or bleeding
  • Progression of slippage or later need for fusion after decompression
  • Nonunion, screw loosening, or adjacent-level degeneration after fusion
  • Persistent pain

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Your individual risks will be discussed in detail during your assessment.

When should I see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Persistent back pain or pain radiating into the leg
  • Symptoms that worsen while walking or a decreasing walking distance
  • Leg weakness or numbness

Frequently asked questions

Does spondylolisthesis always require surgery?

No. Mild slippage can often be managed with non-surgical treatment. Surgery may be considered when pain does not respond to non-surgical care or when there is nerve compression, weakness, or instability.

Can spondylolisthesis be treated endoscopically?

Endoscopic nerve decompression may be performed in selected patients, especially those with low-grade slippage and no instability. Fusion may be needed when instability or progressive slippage is present. The decision is based on examination and imaging.

What is fusion, and why is it needed?

Fusion stabilises moving or slipped vertebrae with screws and a cage so the bones can join. It may be needed to stabilise the spine when there is instability or progressive slippage.

Can I exercise with spondylolisthesis?

Many patients with mild slippage can continue their activities with medical guidance; contact and strenuous sports may need to be limited. Individual advice is provided after assessment.

Can spondylolisthesis progress?

Most degenerative slips progress slowly or remain stable. Regular follow-up is important for younger patients and those with isthmic spondylolisthesis; X-rays can be used to monitor progression.

When can I return to normal activities after surgery?

Recovery after decompression may take days to weeks; recovery after fusion usually takes longer, often months. Timing depends on the patient and procedure and is guided by the clinician.
