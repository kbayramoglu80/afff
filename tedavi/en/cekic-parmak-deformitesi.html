Hammertoe Deformity: Symptoms, Diagnosis, and Treatment

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is a hammertoe? A hammertoe is a deformity in which the middle joint of one of the lesser toes bends. Friction against footwear may cause a corn, pain, or difficulty wearing shoes. Non-surgical options may help while the toe remains flexible; surgery may be considered if the deformity becomes fixed and symptoms persist.

What causes it?

  • Narrow, tight, or high-heeled shoes
  • Imbalance in the toe muscles and tendons
  • Association with hallux valgus (a bunion); the second toe is often affected
  • Foot structure, such as a high arch (pes cavus), flatfoot (pes planus), or a long second toe
  • Less commonly, neuromuscular or joint conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis

Symptoms

  • Bending at the middle joint of the toe
  • Corns and pain over the bent joint or at the toe tip
  • Redness and swelling caused by friction against footwear
  • Pain under the base of the toes (on the sole)
  • Difficulty wearing shoes or walking

How is it diagnosed?

Diagnosis is based on examination. The key distinction is whether the toe can be straightened by hand: a flexible deformity can be corrected manually, while a rigid deformity cannot. Standing X-rays help assess the bones and joints.

Treatment options

ConditionManagement
Flexible hammertoeWide, deep-toe-box shoes, silicone pads or spacers, insoles, and toe-stretching and strengthening exercises. Soft-tissue procedures such as tendon release or transfer may be considered if symptoms persist
Rigid hammertoeNon-surgical options may relieve symptoms. Surgery may reshape the joint (joint resection) or fuse it; the toe may be temporarily stabilised with a wire

The deformity can become rigid over time, so assessment when symptoms begin is important. Hammertoe often occurs with hallux valgus, so the whole foot should be assessed.

Recovery after surgery

After surgery, the foot is protected for a period with special footwear or a dressing. If a wire is used, it is removed after a set period. Swelling usually decreases over several weeks. Recovery time and restrictions depend on the procedure.

Potential risks

  • Recurrence of the deformity
  • Toe stiffness or shortening, or a “floating toe” that does not touch the ground
  • Altered sensation or circulation problems in the toe
  • General surgical risks such as infection or bleeding
  • Wire-related problems, such as pin-site infection or wire migration

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and regular follow-up.

When should I see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • A bent toe or toe pain
  • A persistent corn, sore, or friction despite wearing wider shoes
  • The toe cannot be straightened by hand
  • A toe sore or colour change in someone with diabetes or circulation problems: seek care without delay

Frequently asked questions

Does a hammertoe correct on its own?

Hammertoe does not correct itself and may progress and become rigid. In the flexible stage, appropriate footwear, pads, and exercises may relieve symptoms and help slow progression.

Can exercises correct a hammertoe?

Stretching and strengthening may help when the toe is flexible but may not correct an established deformity on their own. Exercise does not correct a rigid deformity.

When is surgery considered for hammertoe?

Surgery may be considered if pain, corns, or sores persist despite non-surgical treatment, or if a rigid toe makes it difficult to wear shoes. The decision is based on examination.

How long does a wire stay in after surgery?

If a wire is used, the duration depends on the procedure and healing and is determined by the clinician. Some techniques do not use a wire.

Is hammertoe associated with hallux valgus?

Yes. In people with a bunion, the second toe may also develop a hammertoe. The whole foot is assessed, and both problems may be treated together when appropriate.

Can children develop hammertoe?

Toe deformities can occur in children, and some may improve with growth. Paediatric orthopaedic assessment is recommended for pain, friction, or progressive deformity.
