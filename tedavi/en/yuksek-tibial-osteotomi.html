High Tibial Osteotomy (HTO): Joint-Preserving Knee Surgery

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Mesut Kariksiz, Orthopaedic Surgeon · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is high tibial osteotomy (HTO)? High tibial osteotomy (HTO) is a joint-preserving operation in which the upper tibia is cut and realigned to shift the load from the arthritic part of the knee toward the healthier part. It may be used to delay knee replacement in younger, active patients with bowlegs (varus alignment) and osteoarthritis on the inner side of the knee. The patient’s own joint is preserved.

When is it performed?

  • Osteoarthritis on the inner side of the knee (medial compartment) and bowleg (varus) deformity
  • Leg malalignment associated with cartilage damage, meniscal loss, or ligament insufficiency
  • Younger, active patients who wish to preserve their own knee rather than have a replacement
  • Insufficient benefit from non-surgical treatments

HTO is generally not suitable when osteoarthritis is widespread across the knee, inflammatory joint disease is present, or there is marked stiffness. Suitability is determined by examination and standing full-length leg X-rays.

Symptoms

  • Pain on the inner side of the knee that worsens with walking, stairs, or standing
  • Bowleg appearance
  • Swelling after activity and knee stiffness
  • Tenderness on the inner side of the knee

Diagnosis and planning

Leg alignment is measured using an examination, standing full-length leg X-rays, and knee X-rays. MRI shows the condition of the cartilage, menisci, and ligaments. Preoperative planning determines the amount of correction.

How is surgery performed?

  1. Anesthesia: General or spinal anesthesia. Knee arthroscopy may also be performed during the same operation to assess the joint, if needed.
  2. Bone cut: The upper part of the shinbone is cut at the planned angle.
  3. Correction: The bone is opened to correct leg alignment (open-wedge technique) or a piece is removed to close the gap (closed-wedge technique).
  4. Fixation: The bone is fixed with a special plate and screws; bone graft or a bone substitute may be used if needed.

Recovery

  • Crutches and partial weight-bearing may be needed until the bone heals
  • Physical therapy helps restore knee movement and muscle strength
  • Returning to daily activities may take weeks; full recovery and return to sports may take months
  • The plate and screws may be removed after bone healing if they cause discomfort

Possible risks

  • Delayed bone healing or nonunion
  • Loss of correction, undercorrection, or overcorrection
  • Infection, bleeding, and blood clots
  • Nerve or blood vessel injury (rare)
  • Unintended fracture near the bone cut; change in kneecap position
  • Persistent pain

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, preoperative preparation, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Your individual risks will be discussed during your assessment.

When should you see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Bowlegs and persistent pain on the inner side of the knee
  • A diagnosis of knee osteoarthritis at a younger age
  • Worsening pain on the inner side of the knee after meniscus surgery
  • Active patients who wish to delay knee replacement

Frequently asked questions

Who may be a candidate for HTO?

HTO may be considered for younger, active patients with osteoarthritis on the inner side of the knee and bowleg alignment when non-surgical treatment has not helped enough. Suitability is determined by examination and standing full-length leg X-rays.

How does HTO differ from knee replacement?

HTO preserves the patient's own joint and shifts load by correcting leg alignment; knee replacement replaces joint surfaces with implants. HTO aims to delay replacement in younger, active patients.

When can I walk after HTO?

Walking with crutches and partial weight-bearing usually begins early. The physician determines when full weight-bearing is appropriate based on bone healing; this may take several weeks.

How long can HTO delay knee replacement?

The time before knee replacement may be needed varies with the patient's age, the extent of osteoarthritis, and the success of the correction. Some patients may gain years, but the duration varies and cannot be guaranteed.

Is the plate removed after HTO?

The plate and screws may be left in place if they do not cause problems. If they cause pain or tenderness, they may be removed after the bone has healed.

Can I return to sports after HTO?

A gradual return to sports may be possible once the bone has healed and muscle strength is adequate. The appropriate sport and timing are determined individually by the physician.
