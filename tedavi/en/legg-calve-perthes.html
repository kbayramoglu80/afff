Legg-Calvé-Perthes Disease: Hip Pain and Limping in Children

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease? It is a childhood condition in which the blood supply to the femoral head is temporarily disrupted, causing bone tissue to die and then gradually remodel. It most often affects boys aged 4–8 and may cause limping and hip, thigh, or knee pain. Treatment aims to preserve the femoral head's round shape and position in the hip; the condition may last for years.

What causes it?

The blood supply to the femoral head is temporarily disrupted, causing bone tissue to die (avascular necrosis). The exact cause is unknown. The body gradually remodels the bone, but the femoral head may flatten during this process and hip congruence may be affected. It is not caused by anything the child or family has done.

Symptoms

  • A limp, often beginning with no or only mild pain
  • Pain in the groin, thigh, or knee (hip pain may be felt in the knee)
  • Limited hip movement, particularly spreading the leg and rotating it inward
  • Pain that increases with activity and eases with rest
  • In long-standing cases, the leg may become thinner and shorter

How is it diagnosed?

Diagnosis is based on examination and hip X-rays (front and side views); X-rays may appear normal early in the condition. MRI can help with early diagnosis and show the extent of the blood-supply disruption. The disease progresses through stages: initial, fragmentation, reossification, and healing. Treatment decisions depend on the child's age, disease stage, and pattern of femoral-head involvement.

Treatment options

ConditionApproach
Young child with mild involvementObservation; activity modification (reducing high-impact activities such as jumping and running), physical therapy to preserve hip movement, and pain relief if needed
Restricted movement and painRest, physical therapy, sometimes short-term reduced weight-bearing, and exercises to maintain range of motion
Older child or extensive involvement, with the femoral head moving out of the socketSurgery such as a femoral or pelvic osteotomy to improve containment of the femoral head in the socket
Residual deformity after healingIf the femoral head is flattened or hip impingement develops, later hip surgery may be considered (for example, hip arthroscopy or osteotomy)

Hip shape in adolescence and adulthood depends largely on the age at disease onset and the extent of femoral head involvement. Outcomes are generally better when the disease begins at a younger age.

Treatment process

  • The condition may last for years; the child is monitored regularly with examinations and X-rays
  • If surgery is needed, the hip is protected in a cast or brace for a time, followed by physical therapy
  • School and sports activities are adjusted according to the doctor’s advice
  • It is important to keep the family informed throughout the process and provide psychological support to the child

Possible risks and expectations

  • Flattening of the femoral head and loss of hip congruence
  • Hip pain, impingement, and restricted movement
  • Risk of early osteoarthritis in adulthood
  • Mild leg-length difference
  • Surgical risks such as infection, bleeding, and problems with bone healing

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Your child’s individual risks will be discussed in detail during the assessment.

When should you see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Unexplained limping in a child
  • Groin, thigh, or knee pain
  • Restricted outward and inward movement of the leg
  • Increasing pain or limping in a child previously diagnosed with Perthes disease

Frequently asked questions

Does Perthes disease heal on its own?

Perthes disease tends to heal over several years as it progresses through its stages, but the femoral head may become misshapen during bone remodelling. Monitoring and treatment when needed are therefore important.

How long does Perthes disease last?

The disease usually lasts for years. Its duration varies with the child’s age and the extent of the disease. Regular appointments are used to monitor its stage.

When is surgery needed for Perthes disease?

Surgery is generally considered for older children or for extensive involvement when the femoral head moves out of the socket. Observation and physical therapy are usually sufficient for younger children and mild involvement.

Can a child with Perthes disease play sports?

High-impact activities such as jumping and running may be limited during the disease. Activities that place less stress on the hip, such as swimming, are often recommended. Your doctor will provide specific advice.

Can Perthes disease cause lasting problems?

Many children have good outcomes, but flattening of the femoral head can lead to hip pain, impingement, and early osteoarthritis later in life. The age at onset and extent of involvement affect the outcome.

My child has knee pain. Could it be coming from the hip?

Yes. Hip conditions often cause pain that is felt in the knee. The hip should also be assessed if a child has unexplained knee pain or limping.
