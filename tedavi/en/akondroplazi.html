Achondroplasia (Short Stature): Orthopaedic Issues and Care

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is achondroplasia? Achondroplasia is the most common form of short stature, caused by a change in the FGFR3 gene that affects bone growth. The arms and legs are short in proportion to the torso; leg bowing, spinal stenosis, and kyphosis may also occur. Care is multidisciplinary. Orthopaedic treatment is planned according to age, symptoms, and preferences.

What is achondroplasia?

Achondroplasia is a genetic condition that affects the conversion of cartilage into bone at the growth plates. In most cases it results from a new gene change (mutation), although it can also run in families. Intelligence is typically unaffected. The term short stature is used here as respectful, medically appropriate language.

Common orthopaedic problems

AreaProblem
Arms and legsShorter limbs in proportion to the torso, especially the thighs and upper arms
LegsOutward bowing (genu varum), knee laxity, and ankle deformity
SpineThoracolumbar kyphosis, increased lumbar lordosis, and spinal stenosis that may compress nerves
HipsRestricted movement and hip deformity
Head and neckNarrowing at the cervical spine or skull base, which may require neurological assessment

Symptoms and when to suspect it

  • Short arms and legs or a relatively large head at birth or during infancy
  • Delayed motor development
  • Fatigue while walking, leg numbness, or weakness (possible signs of spinal stenosis)
  • Progressive leg bowing or knee pain
  • May be detected on prenatal ultrasound

Children with achondroplasia may need separate neurological monitoring for spinal cord or skull-base problems. Orthopaedic care should be coordinated with paediatric, genetics, neurosurgery, and endocrinology teams.

How is it diagnosed?

Diagnosis is usually based on examination, body proportions, and X-rays; it can be confirmed with genetic testing. Orthopaedic follow-up may include assessment of leg alignment, spinal X-rays, and MRI when needed.

Treatment options

ProblemManagement
Leg bowingGuided growth during childhood; osteotomy after growth is complete or for severe deformity
Short stature or limb lengthLimb-lengthening surgery may be discussed with the patient and family in light of age and personal preferences; multiple stages may be needed
Spinal stenosisWhen symptoms are present, decompression with a neurosurgical or spine team; fusion may be considered in selected cases
MedicationNew medicines related to growth are being developed; the endocrinology and genetics teams can provide current guidance
Supportive carePhysiotherapy, weight management, and adaptive or ergonomic measures

What to expect from limb lengthening

  • Lengthening is a prolonged process and is often performed in stages (for example, the legs first and the arms later)
  • It may offer functional benefits, such as improved reach and ease of self-care, but results vary
  • Several months of external fixation, physiotherapy, and pain management may be required
  • Complications can include infection, joint stiffness, and problems with bone healing
  • Psychosocial support and care at an experienced centre are important before deciding

Potential risks

  • Infection, bone-healing problems, joint stiffness, and nerve or blood-vessel injury after lengthening surgery
  • Incomplete correction or recurrent deformity after osteotomy
  • Nerve injury, dural tear, or infection after spine surgery
  • Anaesthesia risks; airway and spinal anatomy require particular care in achondroplasia
  • The need for more than one operation

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Your individual risks will be discussed in detail during your assessment.

When should I see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Short arms or legs in a child, or concern about delayed growth
  • Leg bowing or knee pain
  • Leg numbness or weakness while walking, or a reduced walking distance
  • Spinal curvature or back pain
  • An assessment to discuss limb-lengthening surgery

Frequently asked questions

Are achondroplasia and dwarfism the same thing?

Achondroplasia is the most common form of short stature sometimes referred to as dwarfism. “Short stature” or “achondroplasia” is preferred here as respectful, medically appropriate language.

Is achondroplasia hereditary?

Most cases result from a new gene change, but achondroplasia can be inherited. Genetic counselling is recommended for family planning.

Can limb lengthening be considered for achondroplasia?

Yes. Limb-lengthening surgery may be considered for selected patients. It is a lengthy and demanding process, and the decision should be made jointly by the person, family, and experienced care team after discussing expectations and risks.

When does leg bowing require surgery?

Correction may be considered if bowing progresses, causes knee pain or walking problems, or significantly affects alignment. Options include guided growth in children and osteotomy after growth is complete.

Why can spinal problems occur in achondroplasia?

Congenital narrowing of the spinal canal and spinal curvature can compress nerves. Difficulty walking or leg numbness should be assessed by a clinician.

What are life expectancy and intelligence like in achondroplasia?

Intelligence is typically unaffected, and many people with achondroplasia lead active, productive lives. Regular follow-up and early management of complications are important; individual care should be discussed with the paediatric and genetics teams.
