Plantar Fasciitis (Heel Pain and Heel Spurs): Symptoms, Diagnosis, and Treatment

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Mesut Kariksiz, Orthopaedic Surgeon · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What are plantar fasciitis and heel spurs? Plantar fasciitis is irritation and degeneration of the plantar fascia near its attachment to the heel bone, where the tissue extends toward the toes. It is one of the most common causes of heel pain. A typical symptom is pain beneath the heel with the first steps in the morning. A heel spur is a bony projection on the heel bone and is often not the actual cause of pain. Treatment is usually non-surgical.

Are plantar fasciitis and heel spurs the same thing?

Seeing a heel spur on an X-ray does not mean that it is the cause of the pain. Treatment targets the actual source of pain, not the spur itself.

Causes

  • Repeated loading, such as prolonged standing, running, and jumping
  • Excess body weight
  • Tight calf muscles (Achilles tendon)
  • Foot shapes such as flat feet (pes planus) or high arches (pes cavus)
  • Unsupportive or hard-soled shoes; walking on hard surfaces
  • Age; more common between 40 and 60 years

Symptoms

  • With the first steps after getting out of bed in the morning, a sharp pain beneath the heel
  • Pain with the first steps after sitting for a long time
  • Tenderness beneath or along the inner side of the heel
  • Pain that worsens with prolonged standing or walking
  • The pain often eases after a few steps but may return later in the day

Night pain, numbness or burning, marked swelling, fever, or sudden pain after an injury may indicate another cause (such as nerve compression, a stress fracture, or inflammation) and should be assessed promptly.

How is it diagnosed?

Diagnosis is based on the history and examination, including assessment of the tender point beneath the heel and foot structure. X-rays can show heel spurs and other bone problems. Ultrasound or MRI may be used to assess thickening of the plantar fascia and other possible causes, such as a stress fracture or soft-tissue problems.

Treatment options

OptionHow it is applied
Activity modificationReducing prolonged standing, running, and walking on hard surfaces if these worsen pain; weight management
Stretching exercisesStretching and strengthening exercises for the plantar fascia and calf (Achilles) muscles, guided by a physician or physiotherapist
Orthotics and footwearSilicone or custom orthotics that support the heel and arch; cushioned, supportive footwear
Medication and cold therapyAnti-inflammatory medication (as advised by a physician) and ice to help reduce pain
Night splintKeeps the fascia stretched overnight to help reduce morning pain
Additional treatmentsShock wave therapy (ESWT), ultrasound-guided injections, and orthobiologic treatments (e.g., PRP) may be considered for selected patients; effects vary across studies and individuals
Surgical treatmentFor selected patients who do not respond to months of non-surgical treatment, procedures such as partial release of the plantar fascia may be considered; associated nerve compression may also be addressed

A corticosteroid injection may provide short-term relief, but it carries risks such as weakening or rupture of the fascia and thinning of the heel fat pad; it is therefore considered only selectively.

How long does recovery take?

Plantar fasciitis improves with non-surgical treatment in many patients, but recovery may take weeks to months. Following the treatment plan and adjusting footwear and activity habits may help reduce recurrence.

Potential risks (surgery and injection)

  • Persistent or recurrent pain
  • General surgical risks such as infection, bleeding, and nerve injury
  • Changes in the foot arch or pain along the outer edge of the foot if too much of the fascia is released
  • Fascia rupture and thinning of the heel fat pad related to corticosteroid injection

When should you see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Heel pain lasting more than a few weeks
  • Pain that worsens despite self-care measures
  • Night pain, numbness, burning, swelling, or redness
  • Inability to bear weight on the heel after an injury

Frequently asked questions

Is a heel spur the cause of the pain?

Usually not. A heel spur may be visible on an X-ray, but heel pain is more often caused by irritation of the plantar fascia. Some people have a spur without pain.

Can heel pain go away on its own?

Plantar fasciitis improves over time with consistent treatment in many patients, although this may take weeks or months. If pain persists or worsens, seek an assessment and a treatment plan.

Is surgery needed for a heel spur?

Most patients do not need surgery. It may be considered for selected patients who do not respond to months of non-surgical treatment, after assessing the actual source of pain.

What exercises are used for plantar fasciitis?

Stretching and strengthening exercises for the plantar fascia and calf (Achilles) muscles are commonly used. A physician or physiotherapist can advise on performing them correctly.

Can orthotics help heel pain?

Heel and arch-supporting orthotics may help reduce pain for many patients. The type should be selected according to the structure of the foot.

Which doctor should I see for heel pain?

Consult an orthopaedic specialist for heel pain. The cause is determined by examination and, if needed, imaging; treatment is planned accordingly.
