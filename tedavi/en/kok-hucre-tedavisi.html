Stem Cell Therapy in Orthopaedics: Nedir, Kanıt Conditionu ve Yasal Çerçeve

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is stem cell therapy in orthopaedics? Ortopedide kök hücre tedavisi, çoğunlukla kemik iliği veya yağ dokusundan elde edilen ve çeşitli hücrelere dönüşebilme potansiyeli taşıyan hücrelerin hasarlı eklem, kıkırdak veya kemik bölgesine uygulanmasıdır. Knee osteoarthritis, kıkırdak hasarı ve avasküler nekroz gibi durumlarda araştırılmaktadır. Bazı çalışmalarda ağrı ve fonksiyonda düzelme bildirilmiştir; ancak çalışmalar sınırlıdır, kıkırdağın yeniden oluştuğu kanıtlanmış değildir ve uygulama yasal düzenlemelere tabidir.

Important information

Stem cell therapy is not a standard treatment recommended for every patient. Procedures must comply with applicable regulations and approvals. This page is for general information; claims such as “a definitive cure” or “regenerates cartilage” have not been scientifically established.

What are stem cells, and where do they come from?

  • Mesenchymal stem cells: cells with the potential to develop into bone, cartilage, and fat tissue
  • Kemik iliği: a concentrate obtained by processing bone marrow, usually collected from the pelvic bone (e.g., BMAC)
  • Yağ dokusu: cell-containing products derived from fat collected from the abdomen or thigh
  • Kültürlenmiş (çoğaltılmış) hücreler: products expanded in a laboratory; these require industrial production and marketing authorization

What conditions are being studied?

  • Knee osteoarthritis (özellikle hafif ve orta evre)
  • Focal cartilage defects (as an adjunct to surgery)
  • Avascular necrosis (impaired blood supply to the femoral head; usually alongside core decompression)
  • Non-union fractures and some tendon disorders

Regulatory framework

Products based on human cells and tissues are subject to applicable regulations in Türkiye. Under the Regulation on the Licensing of Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products, unlicensed advanced therapy medicinal products may not be marketed. Products prepared in a hospital for an individual patient using a non-routine method are considered within the framework of a clinical study authorized by the Ministry. Therefore, whether a stem cell procedure is appropriate depends on the product source, preparation method, and legal status.

Who may not be suitable?

  • People with an active infection or a skin infection at the treatment site
  • People with active cancer or a history of cancer (requires medical assessment)
  • People with bleeding or clotting disorders or taking blood thinners (requires medical assessment)
  • Pregnancy
  • Benefits may be limited in joints with advanced osteoarthritis (bone-on-bone contact)

How is the procedure performed?

  1. Preparation: Examination, imaging (MRI/X-rays), and assessment of suitability.
  2. Cell collection: Bone marrow or adipose tissue is collected and processed under local or light anaesthesia.
  3. Application: The prepared product is injected into the affected joint or area, usually under imaging guidance, or applied during surgery.
  4. Aftercare: Observation and activity guidance; physical therapy is planned.

Evidence to date

Some studies have reported improvements in pain and function after stem cell procedures. However, the studies involve limited numbers of patients, and cell sources, preparation methods, doses, and protocols vary. Long-term data are also limited, and structural regeneration of cartilage has not been clearly demonstrated. Stem cell therapy should not replace standard treatments, but may be assessed for selected patients within an appropriate framework.

Possible side effects and risks

  • Temporary pain, swelling, and tenderness at the injection site
  • Infection (rare)
  • No expected benefit or only a short-lived effect
  • Bleeding and bruising
  • Pain, bleeding, and bruising at the cell collection site (pelvic bone or adipose tissue)
  • Uncertainty about cartilage regeneration and unknown long-term outcomes
  • Safety risks associated with unregulated or non-standardized products

Although orthobiologic treatments are described as non-surgical methods that use a person’s own tissues, they do not have the same effect for every condition or patient. Expectations should be realistic, and treatment decisions should be based on examination and imaging findings.

When should you see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Persistent knee or hip pain despite non-surgical treatment
  • If you have been diagnosed with cartilage damage or avascular necrosis
  • If you would like to know whether stem cell therapy may be suitable for you
  • If you would like a second opinion about a stem cell procedure suggested elsewhere

Frequently asked questions

Can stem cell therapy cure knee osteoarthritis?

Some studies have reported improvements in pain and function, but there is no evidence that osteoarthritis is eliminated or cartilage is regenerated. Responses vary from person to person, and any effect may be temporary.

Can stem cell therapy replace surgery?

No. Surgery may be needed for advanced osteoarthritis or structural damage. Stem cell procedures may be considered for selected patients within an appropriate framework.

What are the sources of stem cells?

Bone marrow, usually from the pelvic bone, or adipose tissue is most often used in orthopaedics. Products expanded in a laboratory require industrial production and authorization.

Is stem cell therapy safe?

When a patient’s own cells are used, the risk of an allergic reaction is low; however, risks include infection, bleeding, and pain at the collection site. Long-term safety and effectiveness data are limited.

Is stem cell therapy permitted in Türkiye?

No. Products based on human cells and tissues are regulated, and unlicensed advanced therapy products may not be marketed. Ask about the legal basis of the procedure and the source of the product used.

What should I ask my doctor before a stem cell procedure?

Ask your doctor about the product’s source and preparation, legal basis and authorizations, scientific evidence, potential risks, expected benefits and limitations, and costs. If you do not receive clear answers, consider seeking a second opinion.
