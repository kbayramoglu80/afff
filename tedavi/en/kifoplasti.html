Kyphoplasty: Balloon Treatment for Spinal Compression Fractures

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is kyphoplasty? Kyphoplasty is usually used for osteoporotic spinal compression fractures. A balloon is inserted into the fractured vertebra through a small incision and inflated; bone cement is then placed in the space created. The aim is to reduce pain and stabilize the fracture. It may be considered after assessing fracture age, pain severity, and imaging. The underlying osteoporosis must also be treated.

When is it performed?

  • Pain associated with an osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture
  • Pain that persists despite pain relief and rest, or that causes substantial limitation
  • Imaging (such as MRI) showing a recent, active fracture that corresponds to the pain
  • Selected fractures related to some tumours, assessed together with the oncology team

Not every spinal fracture requires kyphoplasty. Pain improves over time with rest, medication, or a brace for many patients. The procedure is considered only for appropriate patients and timing.

Symptoms

  • Sudden back or lower-back pain that worsens with movement or coughing
  • Pain after a fall or minor injury, sometimes without a clear injury
  • Loss of height or increasing stooped posture over time
  • Limited movement

Emergency: Seek emergency care immediately for weakness or numbness in both legs, loss of sensation around the groin or anus, or difficulty controlling or passing urine or stool (possible cauda equina syndrome).

How is it diagnosed?

Assessment begins with an examination and X-rays. MRI can help determine whether the fracture is recent and show bone-marrow oedema; CT assesses the fracture structure and integrity of the posterior wall. Bone-density testing (DEXA) and blood tests may be requested to investigate osteoporosis.

How is it performed?

  1. Anaesthesia: Local anaesthesia with or without sedation, or general anaesthesia.
  2. Access: The patient lies face down. Using fluoroscopic guidance, a needle is advanced into the fractured vertebra through a small incision.
  3. Balloon: A balloon is inserted and inflated to create a cavity in the fractured vertebra.
  4. Cement: The balloon is removed and bone cement (PMMA) is injected into the cavity; it hardens within a short time.
  5. After the procedure: Brief observation is typical; many patients stand on the same day or the following day.

Who may not be suitable?

  • People with an active infection
  • People with a bleeding disorder
  • Severe damage to the posterior wall of the vertebra with nerve compression, due to the risk of cement leakage
  • People with progressive neurological deficits, who may need a different operation
  • Cases where the pain does not correspond to the fracture
  • People with an allergy to the cement material

Potential risks

  • Cement leakage: Cement may escape outside the vertebra into the spinal canal, a blood vessel, or a disc; rarely, this can compress a nerve or travel to the lungs (embolism)
  • A new compression fracture in a nearby vertebra, particularly in people with osteoporosis
  • Infection, bleeding, or pain at the needle site
  • Insufficient reduction in pain
  • Allergic reaction and anesthesia risks

These risks are addressed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and follow-up. Your individual risks can be discussed during an assessment.

When should you see an orthopedic specialist?

  • Severe back or lower-back pain that starts after a minor fall or without an obvious injury
  • Inability to move because of pain
  • Loss of height or worsening stooped posture
  • Weakness or numbness in the legs
  • Any of the emergency symptoms listed above

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between kyphoplasty and vertebroplasty?

Both procedures use bone cement. In kyphoplasty, a balloon is first inflated to create a cavity; in vertebroplasty, cement is injected directly. A clinician determines which option is appropriate.

Is kyphoplasty painful?

The procedure is usually performed with local anaesthesia and sedation or with general anaesthesia. Temporary soreness may occur afterwards.

When does the pain improve?

Many patients experience a noticeable reduction in pain within the first few days, although results vary. The procedure is most likely to help when the pain is caused by the fracture.

Can another fracture occur after kyphoplasty?

Osteoporosis can increase the risk of a new fracture in a nearby vertebra. Osteoporosis treatment, vitamin D and calcium when indicated, and fall-prevention measures are important.

Is kyphoplasty suitable for everyone?

No. The fracture generally needs to be recent, the pain should correspond to the fracture, and safety criteria must be met. Non-surgical treatment is sufficient for many patients.

When can I return to normal activities after kyphoplasty?

Most patients start walking within the first few days. Heavy lifting and strenuous activity may be restricted as advised by the clinician; osteoporosis treatment and regular exercise are also recommended.
