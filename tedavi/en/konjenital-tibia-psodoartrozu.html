Congenital Pseudarthrosis of the Tibia: Doğuştan Kaval Kemiği Kaynamama Sorunu

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

Congenital pseudarthrosis of the tibia nedir? Congenital pseudarthrosis of the tibia, çocuğun kaval kemiğinde (tibia) doğuştan var olan eğrilik ve zayıflık nedeniyle kemiğin kırılması ve bu kırığın kaynamamasıdır (sahte eklem). Nadir görülür; olguların önemli bir bölümü nörofibromatoz tip 1 ile ilişkilidir. Tedavi zordur ve çoğu zaman cerrahi gerektirir; amaç kemik kaynamasını sağlamak ve bacağı korumaktır. Tedavi, çocuk ortopedisi ve deformite cerrahisinde deneyimli bir ekiple planlanmalıdır.

What causes it?

The exact cause is not fully understood. A structural abnormality affects the tibia and the surrounding tissue (periosteum), usually in the lower third. The bone is weak, fractures easily, and does not heal on its own. Some cases are associated with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1). It is not caused by anything a parent has done.

Symptoms

  • Forward bowing of the shinbone in infancy or early childhood
  • A fracture after minor trauma or spontaneously; failure of the fracture to heal
  • Leg swelling, abnormal movement, and pain
  • A shorter, thinner leg
  • NF1 signs such as café-au-lait spots on the skin may be present

How is it diagnosed?

Examination and X-rays are the main diagnostic tools and show bowing, bone structure, and the fracture line. MRI or CT may provide further information. Skin and genetic assessment for NF1 may be considered. The stage and associated conditions guide treatment planning.

Treatment options

MethodNotes
Brace (protective support)Used to protect the bowed tibia before a fracture occurs; it may not prevent fractures
Intramedullary nail and bone graftThe fracture site is cleaned, an intramedullary nail is inserted, and bone graft is added
Dış fiksatör (Ilizarov)Compression of bone segments and, if needed, bone transport; may also allow deformity correction
Vascularized fibular graftAn advanced microsurgical procedure transferring bone with its blood supply, used in selected cases
Amputation and prosthesisA last-resort option considered after repeated treatment failure or serious complications, through a shared decision with the family and care team

No single method is successful in every case; fractures may recur even after union. Treatment may require long-term follow-up and more than one operation.

Treatment and follow-up

  • Long-term protection with a cast, brace, or external fixator is needed after surgery
  • Bone healing is monitored with X-rays and may take months
  • Even after union, a protective brace and regular follow-up are needed throughout the growth period
  • Additional treatment may be planned for leg-length difference and ankle deformity

Possible risks and expectations

  • Failure of bone union or refracture after healing
  • Leg-length difference, bowing, and ankle problems
  • Infection, bleeding, and pin-site problems
  • Need for more than one operation
  • Amputation may need to be considered in selected cases

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Your individual risks will be discussed in detail during your assessment.

When should you see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Bowing of the shinbone in an infant or child
  • Fracture after minor trauma or delayed healing
  • Leg bowing or pain in a child diagnosed with NF1
  • Pain, swelling, or new deformity in a previously treated child

Frequently asked questions

What is tibial pseudarthrosis?

It is a false joint that develops when congenital weakness in the shinbone leads to a fracture that does not heal. It is rare in children and often requires surgery.

Is it associated with neurofibromatosis?

Yes. A substantial proportion of cases are associated with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1). Orthopaedic assessment is important if a child with NF1 has a leg deformity.

Is there a definitive treatment?

This is a difficult condition to treat, and no single method works for every child. Union can be achieved in many children, but there is a risk of refracture and further surgery. Long-term follow-up is needed.

When is surgery performed?

Timing depends on age, whether a fracture is present, and bone structure. A protective brace may be used early; surgery may be planned after a fracture.

Can a child walk after treatment?

If union is achieved, most children can walk, although leg-length difference and the need for a brace may persist. Outcomes vary according to the condition of the leg.

Is amputation inevitable?

No. Bone union can preserve the leg in many children. Amputation is discussed with the family and care team in selected cases, such as repeated treatment failure and severe loss of function.
