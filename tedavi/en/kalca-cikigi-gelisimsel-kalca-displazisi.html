Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (DDH) in Infants: Symptoms, Diagnosis, and Treatment

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH)? Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH), commonly called congenital hip dislocation, describes a hip in which the femoral head is loose in its socket, partly or completely displaced, or the socket is shallow. Early cases are usually treated without surgery, often with a Pavlik harness; later diagnosis may require casting or surgery. Infants’ hips should be examined after birth, with ultrasound screening for those at increased risk.

Who is more commonly affected?

  • Girls
  • Babies born in the breech position
  • Babies with a family history of hip dysplasia
  • Firstborn babies, particularly when movement in the womb was restricted (for example, with low amniotic fluid)
  • Tight swaddling that keeps a baby's legs straight and together may adversely affect hip development

A safe position for an infant's hips allows the legs to bend and the hips to rest naturally apart. If swaddling is used, the hips should be able to move freely.

Symptoms

  • Asymmetrical thigh or buttock skin folds
  • One leg appears shorter
  • Limited ability to move the leg outward, especially on one side
  • A “click” felt or heard at the hip during nappy changes
  • Limping or a waddling gait in a child who has started walking
  • Some babies have no symptoms, so routine examination is important

How is it diagnosed?

The baby's hips are examined after birth, including with the Ortolani and Barlow tests. Ultrasound is the preferred method for assessing hip structure during approximately the first 4–6 months and is used for babies at increased risk or with a concerning examination. X-rays are used in older infants and children.

Treatment options

Age / situationManagement
Newborn and early infancyObservation for mild laxity; a Pavlik harness (a soft harness that holds the legs bent and apart) for a dislocated hip or an underdeveloped socket
No response to the Pavlik harness or late diagnosisClosed reduction under anaesthesia followed by a spica cast
Hip cannot be reduced closed or diagnosis is lateOpen reduction; the hip is repositioned surgically, and an osteotomy may be used to reshape the socket or femur
Older child or adolescentOsteotomy to reshape the socket or femur; hip-preserving surgery in selected patients

Early diagnosis and treatment substantially improve outcomes. The earlier treatment begins, the greater the chance of correction without surgery.

Treatment and follow-up

  • The Pavlik harness is worn for the period determined by the clinician; correct harness care and attendance at follow-up visits affect success
  • After casting or surgery, the child may remain in a cast for a time, followed by check-ups and physiotherapy when needed
  • Hip development is monitored with regular examinations and X-rays throughout growth
  • Families should receive guidance on care and daily activities such as bathing and nappy changes

Potential risks

  • Recurrent dislocation or incomplete correction despite treatment
  • Disrupted blood supply to the femoral head (avascular necrosis), particularly after forceful treatment
  • Skin irritation from the harness or, rarely, nerve involvement
  • Infection, bleeding, or problems with bone healing after surgery
  • Insufficient development of the hip socket and early osteoarthritis in adulthood

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Risks relevant to your child will be discussed in detail during the assessment.

When should you see an orthopedic specialist?

  • Asymmetrical leg folds or one leg that appears shorter
  • Difficulty moving the leg outward
  • Risk factors such as family history, breech presentation, or a firstborn girl: ask about ultrasound screening
  • Limping in a child who has started walking

Frequently asked questions

Can DDH resolve on its own?

Mild laxity may resolve on its own during the first weeks, but a dislocated or underdeveloped hip may not correct safely without treatment. If there is concern, an ultrasound and medical examination are needed.

What is a Pavlik harness, and how long is it worn?

A Pavlik harness is a soft device that holds the baby's legs bent and apart to help the femoral head sit in the socket. The clinician determines how long it is worn based on the baby's age and response; treatment usually lasts weeks to months.

When should my baby have a hip ultrasound?

Ultrasound is often recommended during the first weeks or within the first 4–6 months for babies at increased risk, including those with breech presentation, family history, female sex, or a concerning examination. The clinician determines timing and need.

What happens if diagnosis is delayed?

With a late diagnosis, non-surgical treatment is less likely to succeed and casting or surgery may be needed. The risk of early osteoarthritis may also increase. This is why early diagnosis is important.

Can swaddling cause hip dislocation?

Tight swaddling that keeps the legs straight and together may adversely affect hip development. If swaddling is used, the hips and knees should be able to bend and move freely.

Can a child walk normally after treatment?

Most children who receive early, appropriate treatment can walk and remain active normally. Hip monitoring throughout growth is important; late diagnosis or complications may require long-term follow-up.
