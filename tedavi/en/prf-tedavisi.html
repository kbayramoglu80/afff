PRF Therapy (Platelet-Rich Fibrin): What It Is and How It Is Used

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Mesut Kariksiz, Orthopaedic Surgeon · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is PRF therapy? PRF (platelet-rich fibrin) is an autologous product prepared from the patient’s own blood by centrifugation without an anticoagulant. It contains platelets and white blood cells within a fibrin network. Unlike PRP, its fibrin structure is thought to release growth factors more slowly. It may be used for joint injections or as an adjunct during surgery to support tissue healing; evidence in orthopaedics is more limited than for PRP.

How is PRF prepared?

Blood is collected in special tubes and centrifuged at low speed without an anticoagulant. This produces a fibrin network containing platelets and white blood cells. The product may be a liquid (injectable PRF) or a gel/membrane placed on tissue during surgery. The preparation method and speed affect its characteristics.

How PRF differs from PRP

PRPPRF
PreparationUsually with an anticoagulant and two-step centrifugationSingle-step centrifugation at lower speed without an anticoagulant
CompositionPlatelet-rich liquid plasmaPlatelets and white blood cells within a fibrin network
Growth factor releaseReleased more quicklyThought to be released more slowly because of the fibrin network
UseJoint and tendon injectionsInjection or placement on tissue during surgery
EvidenceMore extensively studied in orthopaedicsMore limited in orthopaedics; used more often in dentistry and wound healing

When may it be used in orthopaedics?

  • Intra-articular injection (liquid form) for mild to moderate knee osteoarthritis
  • Use during tendon and ligament repair surgery to support healing (e.g., rotator cuff or anterior cruciate ligament)
  • Selected surgical situations involving bone defects or wound healing
  • Injection in selected cases of tendinopathy

Although orthobiologic treatments are often presented as non-surgical approaches using a patient’s own tissues, they do not have the same effect for every condition or patient. Expectations should be realistic, and treatment decisions should be based on examination and imaging findings.

Who may not be a candidate?

  • People with an active infection or a skin infection at the treatment site
  • Bleeding or clotting disorders, or a very low platelet count
  • People taking blood thinners (medical assessment is needed)
  • Severe anemia or active cancer under treatment (medical assessment is needed)

How is it administered?

  1. Blood draw and preparation: Blood is collected in special tubes and centrifuged.
  2. Preparation: The injection site is cleaned; local anesthesia may be used if needed.
  3. Administration: Liquid PRF is injected into the joint or around a tendon; in surgery, PRF gel or membrane is placed on the tissue.
  4. Aftercare: Brief observation and activity guidance.

Evidence

PRF has been studied less than PRP in orthopaedics. Preparation methods and product characteristics vary across studies, and high-quality evidence for joint injections or surgical support is limited. PRF is therefore considered an additional option for selected patients, not a replacement for standard treatment.

Possible side effects and risks

  • Temporary pain, swelling, and tenderness at the injection site
  • Infection (rare)
  • Lack of expected benefit or short duration of effect
  • Bleeding and bruising

Because PRF is prepared from the patient’s own blood, the risk of allergic reaction is low; however, infection is a risk with any injection or surgical procedure.

When should you see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Knee or tendon pain that persists despite non-surgical treatment
  • If you are considering support for recovery after a tendon or ligament injury
  • Increasing pain, redness, warmth, or fever after treatment

Frequently asked questions

What is PRF therapy?

PRF is prepared from the patient’s own blood without an anticoagulant and contains platelets and white blood cells in a fibrin network. In orthopaedics, it may be used for joint injections or as an adjunct during surgery to support healing.

How does PRF differ from PRP?

PRF is prepared without an anticoagulant and forms a fibrin network; growth factors are thought to be released more slowly. PRP is a liquid product that has been studied more extensively. The appropriate option depends on the condition and treatment goal.

Can PRF help knee osteoarthritis?

PRF may reduce pain in some patients, but evidence for knee osteoarthritis is more limited than for PRP. It does not eliminate osteoarthritis, and responses vary.

Can PRF regenerate cartilage or tendon?

No. It has not been proven to regenerate tissue. It is intended to support the healing environment and reduce symptoms.

How many PRF sessions are needed?

The number of sessions depends on the condition and treatment goal. Your physician will determine the plan after an assessment.

Does PRF have side effects?

Temporary pain, swelling, and tenderness may occur after injection. Since PRF is prepared from the patient’s own blood, allergic reactions are uncommon; infection is rare.
