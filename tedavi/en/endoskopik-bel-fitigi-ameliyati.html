Endoscopic Lumbar Disc Surgery: Candidates and Procedure

Written and medically reviewed by: Doç. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is endoscopic lumbar disc surgery? It is a minimally invasive procedure to remove a herniated disc pressing on a nerve through a small incision using a camera and fine instruments. It may be considered for suitable patients with persistent leg pain or weakness despite non-surgical care. It is not appropriate for every herniation; suitability is determined by examination and MRI.

When may it be considered?

  • Persistent, activity-limiting leg pain (sciatica) caused by a lumbar disc herniation that matches the MRI findings
  • Symptoms that persist despite non-surgical treatment, often for 6–12 weeks (medication, rest and exercise, physiotherapy, and injections when appropriate)
  • Progressive leg weakness or foot drop, which requires prompt assessment
  • Recurrent disc herniation in selected cases

Emergency: Seek emergency care without delay for weakness or numbness in both legs, loss of sensation around the groin or anus, or loss of bladder or bowel control or inability to pass urine (cauda equina syndrome).

Most people with a lumbar disc herniation improve with non-surgical treatment. Surgery is recommended only for suitable patients with an appropriate indication.

Who may not be suitable?

  • People with significant spinal slippage or instability, who may need another procedure such as fusion
  • People with extensive stenosis at multiple levels, or some very large or migrated herniations
  • People with an active infection, a bleeding disorder, or who are not fit for anaesthesia
  • Cases where the symptoms do not match the herniation and pain may have another cause

Preoperative assessment

The clinician assesses pain distribution, muscle strength, and reflexes. A lumbar MRI shows the location of the herniation and nerve compression; standing or dynamic X-rays, CT, or EMG may be requested when needed. Surgery is considered based on the agreement between symptoms, examination, and imaging—not on imaging alone.

How is the surgery performed?

  1. Anaesthesia: General, regional, or local anaesthesia with sedation may be used, depending on the patient.
  2. Approach: A transforaminal (side) or interlaminar (back) approach is selected according to the herniation's location.
  3. Procedure: A camera and instruments are passed through a tube inserted via a small incision. The herniated fragment is removed and the nerve is decompressed while most muscle and bone are preserved.
  4. Closure: The small incision is usually closed with a single stitch or adhesive strips.

The choice depends on the herniation's location, the patient's anatomy, and the surgeon's experience. A smaller incision does not mean a better result for every patient.

Recovery

  • Most patients go home the same day or the following day; the timing varies by procedure and patient
  • Leg pain often improves soon after surgery, while numbness and weakness may take longer to resolve
  • Daily activities and light work can often resume within days to weeks; heavy lifting and strenuous sports should be resumed gradually with medical approval
  • Exercises to strengthen the back and abdominal muscles may help reduce the risk of recurrence

Potential risks

  • Recurrence of the disc herniation at the same level
  • Tear of the dura (the membrane around the nerves) and leakage of cerebrospinal fluid
  • Nerve injury or persistent numbness or weakness (rare)
  • Infection, bleeding, or haematoma
  • Persistent pain or ongoing back pain
  • Conversion to an open procedure during surgery, if needed

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Your individual risks will be discussed in detail during your assessment.

When should I see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Pain radiating down the leg that persists for weeks
  • Weakness or numbness in the foot or leg
  • Night pain that does not improve with rest

Frequently asked questions

Is endoscopic lumbar disc surgery suitable for everyone?

No. Suitability depends on the herniation's location and size, spinal stability, the presence of stenosis, and the patient's symptoms. Conventional microsurgery or another approach may be more appropriate for some people.

When can I return to work after endoscopic disc surgery?

Timing depends on the type of work. People can often return to desk-based work within days to weeks; physically demanding work and sports may take longer. The clinician will advise when it is appropriate to resume activities.

Can a disc herniation recur after surgery?

A disc herniation can recur after any procedure. Strengthening the back and abdominal muscles, using proper lifting technique, and managing body weight may help reduce the risk.

What type of anaesthesia is used?

General, regional, or local anaesthesia with sedation may be used depending on the patient and herniation. The anaesthetist and surgeon determine the approach together.

Must non-surgical treatment be tried first?

Unless there is an emergency such as cauda equina syndrome or progressive weakness, non-surgical treatments such as medication, exercise, physiotherapy, and injections when appropriate are usually tried first. Many patients improve with these measures.

How does endoscopic surgery differ from conventional surgery?

Endoscopic surgery uses a smaller incision and generally causes less muscle disruption. Similar outcomes to conventional surgery have been reported in suitable patients, but the technique is not appropriate for every herniation and surgeon experience matters.
