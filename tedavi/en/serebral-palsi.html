Cerebral Palsy: Orthopaedic Problems and Surgical Treatment

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Mesut Kariksiz, Orthopaedic Surgeon · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What orthopaedic problems can occur with cerebral palsy? Cerebral palsy (CP) is a lifelong condition affecting movement and posture due to an early injury to the developing brain. The brain injury itself does not progress, but musculoskeletal problems may develop over time as a child grows and muscle tone increases (spasticity). These may include joint contractures, hip displacement, scoliosis, and foot deformities. Orthopaedic care is planned through a multidisciplinary approach that may include physical therapy, orthoses, botulinum toxin, and surgery when appropriate.

Why do these problems develop?

  • Spastic muscles remain tight and may not lengthen sufficiently as bones grow
  • Muscle imbalance can gradually change the shape of bones and joints
  • Problems may become more apparent as a child grows, making regular orthopaedic follow-up important
  • The level of mobility (GMFCS classification) helps guide risk assessment and treatment goals

Orthopaedic assessment and follow-up

Orthopaedic assessment includes joint movement, muscle tightness, gait, and posture. Hip X-rays are taken at recommended intervals to detect hip displacement early (hip surveillance). Gait analysis, spinal X-rays for scoliosis, and other imaging may also be used. Assessment is coordinated with pediatric neurology, physical medicine and rehabilitation, orthotics, and therapy teams.

What surgeries may be performed?

  • Soft-tissue surgery: Lengthening or release of shortened muscles and tendons (e.g., Achilles, hamstrings, and hip muscles)
  • Tendon transfers: Restore muscle balance
  • Hip surgery: Soft-tissue procedures and bone cuts (osteotomy) to prevent hip displacement or correct a displaced hip
  • Bone surgery: Correction of rotational and angular leg deformities
  • Spine surgery: Spinal fusion for severe scoliosis (with a separate specialist team)
  • Multilevel procedures: Correction at multiple levels in one session to improve walking in selected children

After surgery

After surgery, the child may be protected in a cast, orthosis, or splint for a period. Physical therapy to support range of motion and muscle strength starts early and continues for months. Goals (walking, sitting, and ease of care) are discussed with the family beforehand and kept realistic.

Possible risks and expectations

  • The deformity may recur, and further surgery may be needed as the child grows.
  • Weakness or a new deformity due to excessive release
  • Infection, bleeding, and anesthesia-related risks (additional health conditions in children with CP require special consideration)
  • Pain, joint stiffness, or problems with bone healing
  • Surgery does not change the brain injury; the aim is to preserve existing function and reduce pain and difficulty with care.

These risks are addressed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Risks relevant to your child will be discussed in detail during the examination.

When should you see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Regular orthopaedic follow-up for a child with CP (especially hip monitoring)
  • Pain, worsening walking, or a new deformity
  • Worsening sitting balance or increased difficulty with care
  • Noticing a curvature of the spine

Frequently asked questions

Can cerebral palsy be cured with surgery?

No. Cerebral palsy results from brain injury and is not cured by surgery. Orthopaedic surgery aims to address muscle and bone problems, preserve function, reduce pain, and make care easier.

Why can hip displacement occur in a child with cerebral palsy?

Muscle imbalance and spasticity around the hip can gradually move the femoral head out of its socket. Regular hip X-rays are therefore taken, and intervention is considered early when needed.

What is botulinum toxin?

It is an injection used to temporarily reduce spasticity in selected muscles. Its effect is temporary, and it is usually used alongside physical therapy and orthoses. The multidisciplinary team decides whether to use it.

Is there an ideal age for orthopaedic surgery?

There is no single ideal age. Timing depends on the child’s age, mobility level, type of deformity, and growth. Some procedures are performed earlier, while others are considered closer to the end of growth.

Will a child be able to walk after surgery?

The aim of surgery is to maintain the child’s current level of walking and improve it where possible. Outcomes vary with the child’s neurological condition and mobility level; realistic expectations are discussed with the family before surgery.

How often should a child with cerebral palsy have orthopaedic follow-up?

Follow-up frequency varies with age and risk. Regular orthopaedic examinations and hip monitoring are recommended; the exact plan is determined by the physician.
