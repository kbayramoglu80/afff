Hip Replacement (Total Hip Arthroplasty): Who It May Help, How It Is Performed, and Recovery

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is hip replacement surgery? Hip replacement (total hip arthroplasty) is surgery in which the femoral head and socket of a damaged hip are removed and replaced with implants made of metal, ceramic, and special plastic. It aims to relieve pain and improve movement caused by hip arthritis, fracture, avascular necrosis, or similar conditions. It may be considered when non-surgical treatments have not helped enough.

When is it performed?

  • Advanced hip osteoarthritis (coxarthrosis)
  • Disrupted blood supply to the femoral head (avascular necrosis)
  • Femoral neck fractures in older adults (partial replacement/hemiarthroplasty may sometimes be used)
  • Residual effects of developmental hip dysplasia, rheumatoid arthritis, and post-traumatic joint damage
  • Pain that interferes with daily activities or sleep, with insufficient relief from non-surgical treatment

Preoperative assessment

  • Examination and pelvic and hip X-rays; CT or MRI when needed
  • Blood tests and cardiopulmonary and anaesthesia assessments
  • Management of chronic conditions, smoking cessation, and weight management where appropriate
  • Preoperative exercises and a plan for returning home

How is the procedure performed?

  1. Anaesthesia: General or spinal anaesthesia. [PLEASE COMPLETE: describe your preferred anaesthesia and pain-management approach.]
  2. Surgical approach: The hip may be reached through different incisions (for example, posterior, anterolateral, or direct anterior). The approach is selected based on the patient and the surgeon's experience.
  3. Preparation: The damaged femoral head is removed, and the socket and femur are prepared for the implants.
  4. Implant placement: The socket and stem components are placed in the bone, and a new head replaces the femoral head. Implants may be cemented or uncemented.
  5. Checks and closure: Movement and leg length are checked, and the wound is closed.

Recovery

  • Most patients begin standing and walking with a walker or crutches within the first few days after surgery
  • Physiotherapy and exercises usually begin soon after surgery
  • Depending on the surgical approach, some movements (such as deep bending or crossing the legs) may need to be avoided temporarily. [PLEASE COMPLETE: add your protocol and typical hospital stay.]
  • Returning to daily activities usually takes weeks, while full recovery may take months
  • Blood-thinning medication and regular follow-up visits are planned

Potential risks

  • Infection
  • Blood clot in the leg or lung
  • Dislocation of the implant
  • Difference in leg length
  • Loosening, wear, or fracture around the implant
  • Nerve or blood-vessel injury (rare)
  • Persistent pain

These risks are addressed through appropriate patient selection, preoperative preparation, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Your individual risks will be discussed during your assessment.

When should you see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Groin or hip pain that interferes with walking or sleep
  • Insufficient relief from non-surgical treatment
  • In an older adult, hip or groin pain and inability to walk after a fall: seek urgent medical care
  • After a previous replacement: pain, fever, redness, or stiffness

Frequently asked questions

Who may need hip replacement?

Hip replacement may be considered for people whose pain and daily activities are affected by advanced hip osteoarthritis, avascular necrosis, a fracture, or another condition, and who have not had enough relief from non-surgical treatment. The decision is based on symptoms, examination, and X-ray findings.

When can I walk after hip replacement?

Most patients begin standing and walking with a walker or crutches within the first few days after surgery. The weight-bearing plan and timing depend on the procedure and are set by the treating surgeon.

How long does a hip replacement last?

Implant longevity varies with the implant, age, body weight, and activity level. Many hip replacements function for years; revision surgery may be needed if an implant loosens or wears over time.

Can a hip replacement dislocate?

Dislocation is possible, and the risk is higher during the early recovery period. Following movement precautions and attending regular follow-up visits can help reduce the risk.

Can there be a leg-length difference after surgery?

Leg length is assessed during surgery and the surgeon aims to achieve an appropriate balance. A small difference may remain and can sometimes be managed with a shoe insert or become less noticeable over time.

What activities can I do after hip replacement?

Many people can return to activities such as walking, swimming, and cycling. A clinician should assess whether running or other high-impact sports are appropriate for the individual.
