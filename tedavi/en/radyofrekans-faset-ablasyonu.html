Radiofrequency Facet Ablation for Facet Joint-Related Back and Neck Pain

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Mesut Kariksiz, Orthopaedic Surgeon · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is radiofrequency facet ablation? Radiofrequency facet ablation applies heat to small nerve branches (medial branches) that carry pain signals from the spine’s facet joints, temporarily interrupting those signals. A diagnostic block is performed first to confirm that the pain comes from the facet joint. Relief often lasts for months, and the procedure may be repeated because the nerve branches can regrow over time. It is not suitable for all back or neck pain.

What is facet joint pain?

Facet joints are small joints at the back of the vertebrae that allow spinal movement. Osteoarthritis, overuse, or injury may cause pain. Typical symptoms include back or neck pain that worsens when bending backward or standing for long periods, may spread to the side, and usually does not extend below the knee.

When may it be considered?

  • Chronic back or neck pain thought to arise from facet joints that persists despite medication, physical therapy, or exercise
  • Patients with a positive response to a diagnostic medial branch block (substantial pain relief)
  • In selected cases of chronic neck pain after a whiplash-type injury

This procedure is not intended for leg pain caused by nerve compression (such as a disc herniation or spinal stenosis). Patient selection and the diagnostic block are important for success.

How is it performed?

  1. Diagnostic block: A local anesthetic is first injected around the nerve branch serving the facet joint; significant pain relief supports the diagnosis of facet-mediated pain.
  2. Preparation: The patient lies face down, and the skin is numbed.
  3. Guidance: Fluoroscopy (live X-ray) is used to place a special needle (cannula) near the nerve branch.
  4. Test: Nerve stimulation is used to confirm positioning and safety.
  5. Ablation: Radiofrequency energy heats the nerve branch for several minutes.
  6. Aftercare: The patient is discharged the same day after brief observation.

Effects and expectations

  • When patient selection is appropriate, substantial pain relief may be expected; duration varies and often lasts months
  • Pain may return as nerve branches can regrow; the procedure may be repeated
  • Temporary increased pain and tenderness at the procedure site may last for days to weeks
  • The procedure does not eliminate the underlying cause (osteoarthritis); exercise and physical therapy may support its effects

Possible risks

  • Pain, bruising, or temporary pain flare at the procedure site
  • Temporary numbness, burning, or tingling
  • Infection (rare) or bleeding (rare)
  • Injury to nearby nerves or a skin burn (very rare)
  • Insufficient pain relief or short-lived effects

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Your individual risks will be discussed during your assessment.

When should you see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Back or neck pain lasting for weeks, especially pain that worsens when bending backward
  • Pain that worsens at night or does not improve with rest
  • Pain accompanied by numbness, weakness, or fever
  • Any of the emergency symptoms listed above

Emergency: Seek emergency care immediately for weakness or numbness in both legs, loss of sensation around the groin or anus, or inability to control or pass urine or stool (cauda equina syndrome).

Frequently asked questions

What is radiofrequency facet ablation?

The procedure applies heat to small nerve branches carrying pain signals from facet joints, temporarily interrupting those signals. A diagnostic block is performed first; ablation is considered for patients who respond.

Is the procedure painful?

The skin and tissue are numbed with local anesthetic. Temporary tingling or muscle twitching may occur during nerve stimulation. Mild pain may last for a few days afterward.

How long do the effects last?

Duration varies and is often several months. Pain may return as nerves can regrow; the procedure may be repeated in suitable patients.

Does radiofrequency treat a herniated disc?

No. Radiofrequency facet ablation is for joint-related pain. Other treatments, such as injections or endoscopic surgery, are used for nerve compression caused by a disc herniation.

Why is a diagnostic block performed?

It helps confirm that the pain truly comes from a facet joint. Substantial relief after the block makes benefit from ablation more likely; ablation is not recommended if there is no response.

When can I return to daily activities?

Most patients return to daily activities the next day or within a few days. Follow your physician’s advice about strenuous activities.
