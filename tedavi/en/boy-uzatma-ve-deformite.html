Limb Lengthening and Deformity Correction Surgery

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 14, 2026

What is limb lengthening and deformity correction surgery? These procedures gradually lengthen or realign a bone after a controlled surgical cut to address a leg-length difference or deformity. Methods may include an external fixator or a lengthening nail inside the bone. The suitable approach is determined individually after examination and imaging.

When may it be considered?

  • Congenital or acquired leg-length discrepancy, for example after a fracture, infection, or growth-plate injury
  • Angular deformities, such as inward or outward bowing or malunion
  • Malunion or nonunion after trauma
  • Walking difficulty, pain, or increased load on the back and neighbouring joints

Not every leg-length difference requires surgery. Small differences can often be managed non-surgically, for example with a shoe insert. Decisions are based on examination and measurements.

Potential risks

  • Infection around external-fixator pins
  • Joint stiffness, limited movement, or muscle and tendon contracture
  • Delayed bone healing or premature consolidation
  • Rarely, nerve or blood-vessel problems

Regular follow-up, pin-site care, and physiotherapy help manage these risks. Risks specific to you will be discussed in detail during your assessment.

When should I see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • A noticeable difference in leg length or limping
  • Pronounced or progressive bowing in one leg
  • Suspected malunion after a fracture
  • One-sided deformity, pain, or walking difficulty in a child

Frequently asked questions

When does a leg-length difference require surgery?

Small differences can often be managed non-surgically, for example with a shoe insert. Surgery may be considered when a larger difference affects walking, the back, or joint health. The decision is based on examination and measurements, including the size and cause of the difference, age, and remaining growth in children.

What is the difference between an external fixator and a lengthening nail?

An external fixator is a frame attached to the outside of the leg and can be adjusted during lengthening; it offers flexibility for deformity correction. An intramedullary lengthening nail is placed inside the bone and leaves no external frame. Suitability depends on the bone, deformity, and individual patient.

How long does lengthening take?

Duration depends on the amount of lengthening, bone-healing rate, and method. The process has two main phases—distraction and bone consolidation—and usually takes months. An individual plan is made after assessment.

What are the risks of limb-lengthening surgery?

Risks can include infection around fixator pins, joint stiffness and limited movement, muscle contracture, delayed bone healing, and, rarely, nerve or blood-vessel problems. Regular follow-up and physiotherapy help manage these risks.

Is physical therapy needed during lengthening?

Yes. Regular exercise and physiotherapy help maintain joint movement and muscle flexibility during lengthening. The clinician and physiotherapist tailor the programme to the patient.

Do children with bowed legs need treatment?

Some leg deformities in children improve as they grow. A paediatric orthopaedic assessment is recommended if the deformity progresses, is one-sided, or causes pain or walking difficulty.
