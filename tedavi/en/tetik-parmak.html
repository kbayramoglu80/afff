Trigger Finger Surgery: Symptoms, Diagnosis, and Treatment

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Mesut Kariksiz, Orthopaedic Surgeon · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is trigger finger? Trigger finger occurs when a tendon that bends the finger catches at the entrance to its sheath on the palm side (the A1 pulley). The finger may lock in a bent position and release with a click when forcibly straightened; symptoms may be more noticeable in the morning. Mild cases may improve with rest, a splint, and corticosteroid injection. A minor procedure to release the sheath may be considered for recurrent symptoms or a locked finger.

Causes

  • Work that requires frequent or forceful use of the fingers and hand
  • Diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and thyroid disease
  • Most common in women and people aged 40–60
  • May occur alongside other hand conditions, such as carpal tunnel syndrome

Symptoms

  • Pain and tenderness at the base of a finger in the palm; a small swelling or nodule may be felt
  • The finger catches or locks in the morning
  • A “click” and sudden release when straightening the finger
  • In advanced cases, the finger may remain bent and need to be straightened by hand
  • The ring finger, thumb, and middle finger are most often affected

How is it diagnosed?

Diagnosis is usually made by examination. A tender nodule at the base of the finger and catching during movement are characteristic. Ultrasound may help assess the tendon and sheath when needed; X-rays or MRI are not routinely required.

How is surgery performed, and what happens afterward?

Surgery is usually a brief, same-day procedure under local anesthesia. The tight sheath is released so the tendon can glide more freely. Finger movement begins early; the wound heals and palm tenderness usually decreases over several weeks. Your physician will advise when to resume heavy gripping.

Possible risks

  • Infection, bleeding, or wound-healing problems
  • Finger nerve injury (rare), with temporary or permanent changes in sensation
  • Finger stiffness or swelling; rarely, bowstringing of the tendon
  • Recurrence or involvement of another finger
  • Temporary injection-related pain, skin lightening or discoloration, and raised blood glucose in people with diabetes

When should you see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • A finger that catches or locks in the morning
  • Persistent pain and a tender lump at the base of a finger
  • A finger that remains bent and must be straightened by hand
  • A child’s thumb remaining bent

Frequently asked questions

Can trigger finger go away on its own?

Mild, recent cases may improve with rest and reduced aggravating activity. Treatment is needed if catching persists or the finger locks.

Does corticosteroid injection help trigger finger?

It is effective as an initial treatment for many patients. Results vary; symptoms may recur, and response may be lower in people with diabetes. Suitability is assessed during an examination.

When is surgery needed for trigger finger?

Surgery may be considered if symptoms recur despite injection, the finger remains locked, or injection is not appropriate.

How is trigger finger surgery performed?

It is usually a same-day procedure under local anesthesia. A small incision is used to release the tight tendon sheath near the base of the finger. Details vary and are discussed before surgery.

When can I use my hand after surgery?

Finger movement begins early. Your physician will advise when to resume heavy gripping, based on healing and the type of work.

Can children develop trigger finger?

Yes. In children, it most often affects the thumb and may be noticed when the thumb stays bent. Treatment differs from that in adults; an assessment by a pediatric orthopaedic specialist is recommended.
