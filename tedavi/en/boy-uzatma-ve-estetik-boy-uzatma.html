Limb Lengthening and Cosmetic Height Surgery: Methods, Process, and Risks

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is limb-lengthening surgery? The procedure increases leg length by cutting the bone and gradually separating it so new bone forms (distraction osteogenesis). It may use an external fixator or a lengthening nail. Treatment takes months and carries a risk of serious complications; cosmetic lengthening requires especially careful discussion of risks and benefits.

When is it performed?

ReasonDescription
Leg-length discrepancyA difference in leg length due to a congenital condition, growth-plate injury, fracture, infection, or disease (medical indication)
Bone-development conditionsShort limbs in conditions such as achondroplasia or hemimelia
Shortening with deformityCorrection of both angular deformity and shortening
Cosmetic reasonsA person with completed growth and a healthy leg wishes to increase their height

Lengthening for a medical reason is different from lengthening solely for cosmetic purposes. Cosmetic lengthening involves surgery on a healthy leg, so the risks must be weighed especially carefully.

Methods

MethodHow it worksNotes
External fixator (circular or monolateral)The bone is cut and gradually lengthened with an external frame attached by pins or wiresAllows a wide range of deformity correction; pin-site care is required
Intramedullary lengthening nailA nail inside the bone lengthens mechanically or magneticallyNo external frame is needed; used in selected patients. Unintended or insufficient lengthening can cause problems
Combined methodsAn external fixator and a nail are used togetherSelected according to the patient and treatment goal

The process: stages

  1. Surgery: The bone is cut (osteotomy) and the lengthening device is placed.
  2. Latency period: Usually several days, allowing the initial healing response to begin.
  3. Distraction period: The bone is gradually lengthened, often at about 1 mm per day; the rate is adjusted to bone formation and the patient's tolerance.
  4. Consolidation period: The new bone is allowed to strengthen; this may take as long as or longer than the lengthening period.
  5. Device removal and rehabilitation: Strengthening and gait training continue.

The full process takes months and depends on the amount of lengthening and the patient's healing. Physiotherapy, regular X-rays, and clinical follow-up are essential throughout.

Cosmetic height lengthening: important considerations

  • It may be considered only for people who have completed growth and are in good general health, after an extensive informed-consent process
  • Expectations should be realistic. The amount of lengthening is limited by the bones, muscles, and nerves; increases are usually limited to a few centimetres
  • Psychological assessment is recommended for people with significant distress about height or concerns about body image
  • People must be prepared for months of restrictions at work and in social life, pain, and demanding rehabilitation
  • Complications may require additional surgery, and the outcome may be worse than before treatment

This page is intended to provide information, not to encourage cosmetic lengthening. The decision should be made in person with a clinician after discussing all risks.

Potential risks

  • Infection, especially at external-fixator pin and wire sites
  • Delayed or absent bone healing, or weak new bone that may fracture
  • Joint stiffness, contracture (knee or ankle), dislocation, or subluxation
  • Nerve or blood-vessel injury, numbness, or muscle weakness
  • Blood clots (thrombosis) or embolism
  • Pain, sleep disruption, and psychological distress
  • Axis deviation in the lengthened leg, or insufficient or excessive lengthening
  • Need for additional surgery or, rarely, permanent loss of function

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Your individual risks will be discussed in detail during your assessment.

When should I see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • You notice a difference in leg length, especially in a child
  • Limping, back or hip pain, or a postural problem
  • Leg shortening after a previous fracture or infection
  • You are considering lengthening and want an initial assessment to discuss the options and risks

Frequently asked questions

How is limb-lengthening surgery performed?

The bone is surgically cut and an external fixator or intramedullary lengthening nail is placed. Gradual lengthening often begins after several days at about 1 mm per day, followed by a period for the new bone to mature.

How long does limb lengthening take?

The full process takes months and depends on the amount of lengthening and the individual's healing. The distraction period is proportional to the planned lengthening, and bone consolidation requires additional time.

Is limb-lengthening surgery painful?

Pain may occur after surgery and during lengthening. Pain relief and physiotherapy are used to manage it, but the severity varies between individuals.

Is cosmetic height lengthening safe?

Cosmetic lengthening is a prolonged operation on a healthy leg with a risk of serious complications. It requires careful assessment, realistic expectations, and a fully informed decision.

Is limb lengthening suitable for everyone?

No. It may be considered for people who have completed growth, are in suitable general health, and have realistic expectations. Assessment differs when there is a medical indication. Suitability is determined by examination, X-rays, and sometimes additional tests.

Will I be able to walk normally after surgery?

Walking aids and physiotherapy are needed during lengthening. Walking returns to normal for many patients after the process is complete, although stiffness or weakness may persist. Rehabilitation is critical.
