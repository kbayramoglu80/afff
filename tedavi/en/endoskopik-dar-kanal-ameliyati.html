Endoscopic Surgery for Lumbar Spinal Stenosis: Symptoms, Diagnosis, and Treatment

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is lumbar spinal stenosis? Lumbar spinal stenosis is narrowing of the spinal canal that compresses nerves, often due to age-related degeneration and ligament thickening. Symptoms may include leg pain or heaviness that worsens with walking and improves when sitting or leaning forward. Selected patients who do not improve with non-surgical care may be considered for endoscopic decompression.

What causes it?

  • Age-related degeneration of the discs and facet joints
  • Thickening of the ligamentum flavum
  • Disc bulges and bone spurs (osteophytes)
  • Spondylolisthesis and a congenitally narrow spinal canal

Symptoms

  • Leg pain, numbness, or heaviness that worsens with walking and improves when sitting or leaning forward (neurogenic claudication)
  • A gradually shorter walking distance; relief when leaning on a shopping trolley
  • Lower-back pain, although this does not occur in everyone
  • Leg weakness or a sense of imbalance
  • May affect both legs

Emergency: Seek emergency care without delay for weakness or numbness in both legs, loss of sensation around the groin or anus, or loss of bladder or bowel control or inability to pass urine (cauda equina syndrome).

How is it diagnosed?

Examination and medical history are important, especially walking distance and symptoms that change with position. A lumbar MRI shows canal narrowing and nerve compression. Standing and dynamic (flexion-extension) X-rays can show slippage or instability. CT or EMG may provide additional information when needed. Vascular causes of leg pain, such as peripheral artery disease, should also be considered.

How is endoscopic decompression performed?

  1. Anaesthesia: General or regional anaesthesia, sometimes with sedation; the method is selected for the patient.
  2. Access: A tube is inserted through a small incision to reach the narrowed area.
  3. Decompression: Using a camera, thickened ligament and bone spurs compressing the nerves are removed to widen the canal. Structures that stabilise the spine are preserved as much as possible.
  4. Closure: The incision is small; most patients get up on the same day or the following day.

Expectations and recovery

  • Leg pain and walking distance improve substantially for many patients, but outcomes vary
  • Back pain may not resolve completely because other conditions may contribute to it
  • Daily activities can often resume within days to weeks; walking and light exercise are encouraged early
  • Degenerative changes continue over time; weight management and muscle strengthening are important

Potential risks

  • Dural tear and cerebrospinal-fluid leakage
  • Nerve injury or persistent numbness or weakness (rare)
  • Infection, bleeding, or haematoma
  • Incomplete decompression or recurrent narrowing at the same or an adjacent level
  • Slippage or instability after decompression, potentially requiring later fusion
  • Conversion to an open procedure

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Your individual risks will be discussed in detail during your assessment.

When should I see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Leg pain or fatigue that worsens while walking and improves when sitting
  • A significant reduction in walking distance
  • Weakness or numbness in the legs

Frequently asked questions

Does lumbar spinal stenosis go away on its own?

Spinal-canal narrowing caused by degeneration does not go away on its own. However, medication, exercise, and physiotherapy can control symptoms for many patients. Surgery may be considered when these treatments do not help enough.

When is surgery considered for spinal stenosis?

Surgery may be considered when walking distance is significantly reduced, pain severely limits daily life, non-surgical treatment has not helped enough, or weakness develops. Symptoms of cauda equina syndrome are an emergency.

Is endoscopic surgery suitable for everyone with stenosis?

No. Suitability depends on the extent of the narrowing, spinal slippage or instability, and the patient's anatomy. Open decompression or fusion may be more appropriate for some patients.

Will I be able to walk farther after surgery?

Leg pain and walking distance improve substantially for many patients, although outcomes vary. Expectations should be discussed after examination and MRI.

Are injections a lasting treatment for stenosis?

No. Injections may temporarily reduce pain but do not remove the cause of the narrowing. They may provide enough relief to support physiotherapy and activity.

When can I walk after surgery?

After endoscopic surgery, patients usually get up and start walking on the same day or the following day. Strenuous activities are resumed gradually with medical approval.
