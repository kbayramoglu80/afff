Slipped Capital Femoral Epiphysis (SCFE): Hip and Knee Pain in Adolescents

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is slipped capital femoral epiphysis? Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE) is the backward and downward slippage of the femoral head from the neck of the femur along the growth plate (epiphysis) in adolescents during their growth spurt. It is most common in children aged 10–16, in overweight children, and in boys. Symptoms include pain in the groin, thigh, or knee, limping, and the leg being turned outward. When diagnosed, the child should stop putting weight on the leg, and surgical treatment is usually required.

In whom is it more common?

  • During the growth spurt (puberty), usually between the ages of 10–16
  • In overweight or obese children
  • In boys
  • It can also occur at unusual ages in some hormonal and metabolic diseases (thyroid, growth hormone problems, kidney diseases).

Symptoms

  • Groin, hip, thigh, or knee pain; knee pain may originate from the hip
  • Limping
  • The leg or foot rests in an outwardly rotated position
  • Limited movement in the hip (especially internal rotation)
  • Sudden severe pain and inability to bear weight may indicate an acute or worsening slip: seek urgent care

If an adolescent presents with unexplained knee or hip pain and a limp, hip X-rays should be considered in addition to a knee examination. A delay in diagnosis may allow the slip to worsen and increase the risk of complications.

How is the diagnosis made?

Diagnosis is based on examination and two-view hip X-rays (anteroposterior and lateral views). The degree and stability of the slip help determine the treatment plan. MRI or CT may provide additional information when needed. Blood tests may be requested to investigate hormonal or metabolic causes.

Treatment options

SituationApproach
First step after diagnosisStop weight-bearing (use crutches or a wheelchair) and obtain urgent orthopaedic assessment
Mild or moderate slipThe femoral head is usually stabilised where it is with one or two screws (in-situ screw fixation) to prevent further slipping
Severe slippage or resulting deformityMore extensive surgery (reduction or femoral osteotomy) to correct the dislocation; surgery for late-onset hip impingement in selected patients
Contralateral hipIn children with one side affected, a slip may also develop in the other hip; monitoring of the opposite side or prophylactic fixation may be considered in selected patients.

Slipped capital femoral epiphysis cannot be treated without surgery. The condition does not improve with casting or rest alone; surgery is necessary to prevent progression of the slip.

Postoperative period

After the surgery, the child moves with crutches for a while, without putting any weight on the leg or with limited weight-bearing. Physical therapy and regular X-ray check-ups are scheduled. The time to return to sports and strenuous activity is determined by the physician based on healing and closure of the growth plate. Weight control is important for long-term outcomes.

Potential risks and outcomes

  • Impaired blood flow to the femoral head (avascular necrosis) and damage to the hip joint cartilage (chondrolysis) are serious complications and occur more frequently when the slip is severe or unstable.
  • Hip impingement, restricted movement, and early osteoarthritis
  • Development of subluxation in the contralateral hip
  • Screw-related problems, infection, or bleeding
  • Leg-length discrepancy

These risks are mitigated through appropriate patient selection, meticulous technique, and regular check-ups. Those that apply to your child will be discussed in detail during the examination.

When should you consult an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Groin, hip, thigh, or knee pain with limping in an adolescent: seek care without delay
  • The leg is held in an outwardly rotated position
  • Sudden severe pain and inability to bear weight: seek emergency care
  • Pain on the opposite side in a child who has previously had SCFE

Frequently asked questions

Does slipped capital femoral epiphysis resolve spontaneously?

No. The slippage does not correct itself and may progress if left untreated. Surgery is required to stop the slippage.

Could knee pain be caused by the hip?

Yes. In adolescent children, pain from hip disorders is often felt in the knee. If there is unexplained knee pain and limping, an X-ray of the hip should also be evaluated.

Should the surgery be performed urgently?

Once the diagnosis is made, the child should stop putting weight on the leg and should be evaluated by an orthopedic specialist as soon as possible. The timing of surgery is determined by the severity and stability of the slip.

Can the child play sports after the surgery?

After the slippage has been stopped and the bone has healed, activities can be gradually resumed. The timing is determined by the physician through X-ray examinations.

Will there be slippage on the other hip as well?

There is a risk of developing dislocation in the contralateral hip, especially in certain hormonal and metabolic conditions and in very young individuals. Therefore, the other hip is monitored, and prophylactic fixation is considered in some patients.

Does being overweight play a role in this disease?

Yes. Excess weight increases the load on the growth plate and raises the risk. Weight control after treatment is important for long-term results.
