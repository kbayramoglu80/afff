PRP Therapy (Platelet-Rich Plasma): Uses and How It Is Administered

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Mesut Kariksiz, Orthopaedic Surgeon · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is PRP therapy? PRP (platelet-rich plasma) is an autologous orthobiologic treatment in which platelet-rich plasma separated from the patient’s own blood is injected into or around a joint or tendon. It may be used for knee osteoarthritis and tendon problems such as tennis elbow and Achilles or patellar tendinopathy. Effects vary by condition, preparation method, and individual. PRP may reduce pain, but it has not been proven to regenerate cartilage or torn tissue.

How is PRP prepared?

A small amount of blood (a few tubes) is collected and centrifuged to separate blood cells from plasma. The platelet-rich portion is prepared for injection. Because it is made from the patient’s own blood, the risk of transmitting infection or causing an allergic reaction is low. Preparation methods and product composition (including platelet and white blood cell levels) vary by system.

When is it used?

  • Knee osteoarthritis (mild to moderate)
  • Tendinopathies: tennis elbow (lateral epicondylitis), Achilles and patellar tendinopathy, and shoulder tendon problems
  • Selected conditions such as plantar fasciitis (heel pain)
  • Some muscle injuries and use as an adjunct to healing after surgery
  • May be considered when non-surgical treatments have not provided sufficient benefit

Although orthobiologic treatments are often presented as non-surgical approaches using a patient’s own tissues, they do not have the same effect for every condition or patient. Expectations should be realistic, and treatment decisions should be based on examination and imaging findings.

Who may not be a candidate?

  • People with an active infection or a skin infection at the treatment site
  • People with bleeding or clotting disorders or very low platelet counts
  • People taking blood thinners (medical assessment is needed)
  • People with a febrile illness, severe anemia, or undergoing cancer treatment (medical assessment is needed)
  • Benefit may be limited in advanced knee osteoarthritis with marked joint-space narrowing

How is it administered?

  1. Blood draw and preparation: The product is prepared promptly.
  2. Preparation: The treatment area is cleaned; local anesthesia may be used if needed.
  3. Injection: PRP is injected into the joint or around the tendon, often with ultrasound guidance to help target the intended site.
  4. Aftercare: Brief observation; most patients go home the same day.

How many sessions are needed, and when might the effects appear?

The number of sessions varies by condition and protocol; one or several sessions may be planned. Effects usually develop over weeks rather than immediately. Avoid strenuous activity for a period after injection, and follow the exercise plan set by your physician.

Evidence

Some studies suggest that PRP may improve pain and function in knee osteoarthritis in the short to medium term. However, preparation methods, product composition, and injection protocols differ across studies, making results difficult to compare. Findings for tendon problems are more variable. PRP has not been proven to regenerate cartilage or torn tissue, so it is generally considered an adjunctive treatment.

Possible side effects and risks

  • Temporary pain, swelling, and tenderness at the injection site
  • Infection (rare)
  • No expected benefit or a short-lived effect
  • Bleeding and bruising
  • A temporary flare of pain lasting a few days after injection (common)

Because PRP is prepared from the patient’s own blood, the risk of an allergic reaction is low; however, infection is a risk with any injection.

When should you see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Knee or tendon pain that persists despite non-surgical treatment
  • Increasing swelling, redness, warmth, or fever after injection
  • If you would like to discuss which orthobiologic treatment may be suitable for you

Frequently asked questions

What is PRP therapy?

PRP is a treatment in which platelet-rich plasma prepared from the patient’s own blood is injected into or around a joint or tendon. It may be used for knee osteoarthritis and tendon problems. Effects vary by condition and individual.

Does PRP treat knee osteoarthritis?

PRP does not eliminate osteoarthritis. In some patients with mild to moderate knee osteoarthritis, it may temporarily improve pain and function; responses vary.

Can PRP regenerate cartilage?

No. Cartilage regeneration has not been proven. PRP is generally intended to reduce pain and support the healing environment.

How many PRP sessions are given?

The number of sessions depends on the condition and protocol; one or several sessions may be planned. Your physician will determine the plan after an assessment.

Is PRP injection painful, and does it have side effects?

Mild discomfort may occur during treatment; local anesthesia may be used if needed. Pain or swelling may last for a few days after injection. Since PRP is prepared from the patient’s own blood, allergic reactions are uncommon; infection is rare.

How does PRP differ from a corticosteroid injection?

Corticosteroids are potent anti-inflammatory medicines that may relieve pain quickly, but their effects are temporary and repeated use may weaken tissue. PRP is a biologic product made from the patient’s own blood; effects may take longer to develop and vary. The appropriate option depends on the condition.
