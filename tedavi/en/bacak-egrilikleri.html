Leg Deformities (Bowlegs and Knock Knees)

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

When do bowlegs or knock knees need treatment? Bowlegs (genu varum) curve outward, while knock knees (genu valgum) angle inward. These patterns can be normal at certain ages and may correct over time. Assessment is advisable if the deformity is one-sided, progressive, painful, affects walking, or persists beyond the expected age. Treatment may involve guided growth or osteotomy.

Normal variation or a medical condition?

AgeExpected pattern
Newborn to age 2Mild bowlegs (genu varum) are normal and usually lessen after a child begins walking
Ages 3–4Knock knees (genu valgum) may appear and are usually part of normal development
After ages 7–8Leg alignment approaches the adult pattern

Physiological leg alignment patterns are usually symmetrical and improve over time. One-sided, pronounced, progressive, or painful deformities should be assessed.

What can cause abnormal leg alignment?

  • Blount disease: A disorder of the upper growth plate of the shinbone that can cause progressive bowing, especially in children who walk early or have a higher body weight
  • Rickets due to vitamin D, calcium, or phosphate deficiency or a metabolic disorder
  • Growth-plate damage after a fracture or infection
  • Bone-development conditions such as achondroplasia
  • Bowlegs associated with knee osteoarthritis in adolescents or adults

Symptoms

  • A noticeable gap between the knees or ankles
  • Waddling while walking, or feet turning inward or outward
  • Knee pain or easy fatigue
  • One-sided deformity or a difference in leg length
  • Inner knee pain in an adult with bowlegs and osteoarthritis

How is it diagnosed?

The clinician examines leg alignment and measures the distance between the knees. A standing full-length leg X-ray shows the mechanical axis and which bone contributes to the deformity. Blood tests (calcium, phosphate, vitamin D, and alkaline phosphatase) may be requested if rickets is suspected. MRI can assess the growth plate and bone when needed.

Treatment options

ConditionManagement
Physiological deformityUsually no treatment is needed; periodic observation may be advised
Underlying metabolic cause (rickets)Vitamin D, calcium, or phosphate treatment with input from paediatric and endocrinology teams
Significant deformity during growthGuided growth: A small plate (eight-plate) is placed on one side of the growth plate to gradually correct alignment as the child grows
Severe deformity or completed growthOsteotomy: The bone is cut and corrected, then stabilised with a plate, nail, or external fixator; leg length may also be adjusted
Adult bowlegs with knee osteoarthritisHigh tibial osteotomy may redistribute load across the knee in selected patients

For physiological alignment patterns, the aim is to avoid unnecessary surgery. Surgery may be considered for progressive or symptomatic deformity and at an appropriate time.

Treatment course

  • After guided-growth surgery, walking usually resumes soon; the plate is removed once alignment is corrected, with regular X-ray follow-up
  • After osteotomy, crutches may be needed for a period. Bone healing takes months, and physiotherapy is planned
  • Alignment is monitored during growth to check for recurrence

Potential risks

  • Incomplete correction or recurrence of the deformity
  • Overcorrection (deformity in the opposite direction) or plate-related problems after guided growth
  • Delayed bone healing, infection, or nerve and blood-vessel injury after osteotomy
  • A change in leg length
  • Knee stiffness or pain

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Your individual risks will be discussed in detail during your assessment.

When should I see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • The deformity is one-sided or differs noticeably between legs
  • Bowlegs worsen after age 2 or persist beyond age 8
  • Knee pain, limping, or difficulty walking
  • Progressive bowlegs in a child with early walking or higher body weight (possible Blount disease)
  • Inner knee pain and bowleg alignment in an adult

Frequently asked questions

Do bowlegs in children correct on their own?

Mild bowlegs in newborns and children up to age 2 are usually part of normal development and improve after walking begins. One-sided, progressive, or pronounced deformity should be assessed.

When do knock knees need treatment?

Knock knees around ages 3–4 resolve on their own in most children. Treatment may be considered if the deformity is pronounced, progressive, one-sided, painful, or does not improve during growth.

What is Blount disease?

Blount disease causes progressive bowing because of a disorder in the upper growth plate of the shinbone. It is more common in children who walk early or have a higher body weight; early assessment is important.

What is guided growth (8-plate)?

A small plate is placed on one side of the growth plate to slow growth on that side and gradually correct alignment. It is inserted through a small incision and removed later.

Can bowlegs in adults be corrected with surgery?

Yes. Osteotomy can correct leg alignment in selected patients. In younger, active patients with knee osteoarthritis, high tibial osteotomy aims to redistribute load across the knee.

Can exercise or insoles correct leg deformity?

Exercise or insoles cannot correct a structural bone deformity. Physiological alignment patterns do not need intervention; pronounced deformities should be assessed and surgery considered if appropriate.
