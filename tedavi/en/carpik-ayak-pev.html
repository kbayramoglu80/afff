Clubfoot (Congenital Talipes Equinovarus): Symptoms, Diagnosis, and Treatment

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is clubfoot (CTEV)? Clubfoot is a deformity present at birth in which the foot turns inward and downward. Standard care uses the Ponseti method: serial casts, Achilles tenotomy in most infants, and then a special shoes-and-bar brace for several years. With early, consistent treatment, most infants do not need extensive surgery.

What causes it?

Clubfoot has no single known cause. Genetic predisposition and some factors during pregnancy may play a role; it is more common in boys and in families with a history of the condition. In some cases, it occurs with another neurological or musculoskeletal disorder. It is not caused by anything the parent has done.

Symptoms

  • The foot turns inward and downward, with the sole facing inward
  • The foot cannot be easily corrected and the ankle is stiff
  • It may affect one or both feet
  • The affected foot and calf may be shorter and smaller
  • Pain is usually absent, but untreated clubfoot can significantly affect walking and shoe wear

How is it diagnosed?

Diagnosis is made by examining the foot at birth. It may be detected on prenatal ultrasound in some babies. X-rays are not usually needed; alignment and stiffness are assessed during examination. It is important to distinguish clubfoot from positional foot deformity, in which a baby's foot turns inward temporarily.

Treatment: the Ponseti method

  1. Serial casting: The foot is gently corrected each week and held in a long-leg cast. The alignment usually improves substantially after several casts.
  2. Achilles tenotomy: In most infants, the Achilles tendon is lengthened through a small incision to correct the heel position, usually under local anaesthesia. Casting continues for several more weeks.
  3. Foot-abduction brace: After casting, special shoes attached to a bar maintain the correction. The brace is worn for most of the day initially, then at night and during the maintenance phase for several years.

The most common cause of recurrence is not following the foot-abduction brace schedule. Consistent adherence to the treatment plan directly affects the outcome.

When is surgery needed?

Surgery may be considered for resistant cases that do not respond to the Ponseti method, recur, or are diagnosed late. Procedures may include soft-tissue release, tendon transfer (such as tibialis anterior transfer), and, when needed, osteotomy. Surgery is not the first choice because it can cause stiffness and pain even when it corrects the foot.

Potential risks and expectations

  • Recurrence of the deformity; the risk increases when the brace schedule is not followed
  • Skin irritation or sores from casting
  • Bleeding or infection after Achilles tenotomy (rare)
  • The treated leg, calf, or foot may remain smaller than the other side
  • Infection, foot stiffness, or pain after surgery

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Risks relevant to your child will be discussed in detail during the assessment.

When should I see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • If a baby's foot turns inward and downward, seek assessment soon after birth, in the first few weeks
  • The foot cannot be easily corrected
  • The deformity returns after treatment
  • A skin sore under a cast, or discolouration or swelling of the toes

Frequently asked questions

Does clubfoot correct on its own?

No. True clubfoot does not correct itself and can significantly affect walking and shoe wear if untreated. Early treatment can produce very good results.

What is the Ponseti method, and how long does it take?

The Ponseti method involves weekly serial casts, a small Achilles tenotomy in most infants, and then several years of wearing special shoes attached to a bar. Casting usually takes weeks; bracing continues for years.

Does clubfoot require surgery?

Most infants do not need surgery when treated with the Ponseti method. Surgery may be considered for resistant, recurrent, or late-presenting cases.

Why are the boots and bar so important?

The shoes and bar help prevent the corrected foot from returning to its previous position. Recurrence is directly associated with not following the bracing schedule, so consistent use by the family is very important.

Can a child walk normally after treatment?

Most children who receive early, appropriate treatment can run, play sports, and wear regular shoes. The affected foot and calf may nevertheless remain somewhat smaller or thinner.

Can clubfoot occur in a later-born sibling?

The chance of clubfoot in a later-born sibling is higher when there is a family history. Foot assessment is therefore recommended during subsequent pregnancies and after birth.
