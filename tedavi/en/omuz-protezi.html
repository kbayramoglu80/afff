Shoulder Replacement (Shoulder Arthroplasty): Anatomical and Reverse Implants

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Mesut Karıksız, Orthopaedics and Traumatology Specialist · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is shoulder replacement surgery? Shoulder replacement is an operation in which damaged joint surfaces are replaced with metal and plastic implants. It aims to reduce pain and improve movement. It may be considered for advanced shoulder osteoarthritis, joint damage caused by a large irreparable rotator cuff tear (cuff arthropathy), and some complex fractures when non-surgical treatment has not helped enough. An anatomical implant may be used when the rotator cuff is intact; a reverse implant may be considered when it is deficient.

When is it performed?

  • Advanced shoulder osteoarthritis and inflammatory joint disease
  • Joint damage due to a large irreparable rotator cuff tear (cuff arthropathy)
  • Bone death due to impaired blood supply to the humeral head (avascular necrosis)
  • Complex, difficult-to-treat shoulder fractures, particularly in older adults
  • Joint damage following previous shoulder surgery or a fracture

Shoulder replacement may be considered when pain and loss of function persist despite non-surgical options such as medication, physiotherapy, exercise, and injections. Not every shoulder problem requires a replacement.

Preoperative assessment

The assessment includes pain, range of motion, and muscle strength. X-rays, CT (to assess the socket bone), and MRI (to assess the rotator cuff) may be used for planning. General health, medications, and other conditions are reviewed with the anaesthesia team; any active infection should be treated before surgery.

How is the surgery performed?

  1. Anaesthesia: General anaesthesia, often combined with a shoulder nerve block to help reduce postoperative pain.
  2. Incision: The joint is accessed through an incision at the front of the shoulder.
  3. Preparation: The damaged humeral head is removed and the socket (glenoid) is prepared.
  4. Implant: Components are placed in the humerus and socket according to the selected implant type.
  5. Closure: The soft tissues are repaired, the wound is closed, and the arm is placed in a sling.

Recovery

  • The hospital stay is usually a few days; the duration varies by patient
  • The arm is protected in a sling for a period, which is gradually reduced as directed by the doctor
  • Physiotherapy often starts with passive movement, followed by active movement and strengthening as advised
  • Returning to daily activities usually takes weeks, while full recovery and strengthening take months
  • Driving and lifting heavy objects should wait for medical approval; heavy lifting and strenuous impact sports may be restricted

Expectations

  • A substantial reduction in pain is the first expected improvement for many patients; movement and function also often improve
  • Improvement in movement depends on the type of replacement and the condition of the rotator cuff and muscles; not every shoulder regains full range of motion
  • Joint replacements can wear over time; longevity depends on the patient’s age, activity, and implant

Possible risks

  • Infection
  • Loosening, wear, or breakage of the replacement, possibly requiring revision surgery
  • Shoulder dislocation (instability), particularly with reverse shoulder replacement
  • Nerve or blood vessel injury, restricted movement, and stiffness
  • Bone fracture (during or after surgery) or problems with the socket component
  • Blood clots, anaesthesia-related risks, and general surgical risks
  • Failure to achieve the expected improvement in pain and movement

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Your individual risks will be discussed in detail during your assessment.

When should you see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Shoulder pain lasting for weeks, worsening at night, or not relieved by medication
  • Significant weakness when raising the arm or loss of movement
  • Persistent pain and loss of function after a shoulder fracture or dislocation
  • Pain, loss of movement, fever, or wound discharge in someone who has had shoulder replacement surgery

Frequently asked questions

Who may be considered for shoulder replacement surgery?

It may be considered for advanced shoulder osteoarthritis, joint damage due to a large irreparable rotator cuff tear, avascular necrosis, and some complex fractures when pain and loss of function persist despite non-surgical treatment.

What is reverse shoulder replacement?

In a normal shoulder, the ball is on the humerus and the socket is on the shoulder blade. In reverse replacement, the ball is placed on the shoulder blade and the socket on the humerus. This allows the deltoid muscle, rather than the rotator cuff, to help raise the arm. It is used when the rotator cuff is deficient.

When can I use my arm after shoulder replacement?

The arm is protected in a sling for a period. Light daily activities usually resume within weeks, while full recovery and strengthening take months. Your doctor and physiotherapist will guide the timing.

How long does a shoulder replacement last?

Longevity varies with age, activity, and the type of replacement. Replacements can wear or loosen over time, so regular follow-up is recommended.

Will there be pain after surgery?

Pain is expected during the first few days after surgery and is managed with a nerve block and pain medication. Pain usually decreases substantially over time; physical therapy is an important part of recovery.

Are there alternatives to replacement?

Yes. Options may include medication, exercise and physical therapy, injections, and— for selected patients—surgical procedures such as arthroscopic debridement, tendon repair, or tendon transfer. The appropriate approach is determined by examination and imaging.
