Unicompartmental (Partial) Knee Replacement: Candidates, Procedure, and Recovery

Written and medically reviewed by: Assoc. Prof. Mesut Kariksiz, Orthopaedic Surgeon · Last updated: November 15, 2026

What is unicompartmental (partial) knee replacement? Unicompartmental knee replacement replaces only the damaged part of the knee, usually the inner compartment, when osteoarthritis is limited to one area; healthy cartilage and the cruciate ligaments are preserved. It is a smaller operation than total knee replacement and may allow faster recovery in selected patients. Careful patient selection is important.

Who may be a candidate?

  • Osteoarthritis affecting only one compartment (most often the inner side of the knee)
  • An intact anterior cruciate ligament and a sufficiently stable knee
  • Knee range of motion and deformity within certain limits
  • No inflammatory joint disease (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis)
  • Insufficient benefit from non-surgical treatments

Suitability is assessed using an examination, standing X-rays, and MRI when needed. Partial replacement may not be suitable if osteoarthritis is widespread.

How does it differ from total knee replacement?

How is surgery performed?

  1. Anesthesia: General or spinal anesthesia.
  2. Preparation: The cartilage and a thin layer of bone in the damaged compartment are removed.
  3. Implant placement: Small metal components are placed on the thighbone and shinbone, with a special plastic insert between them.
  4. Check and closure: Knee stability and movement are checked, and the incision is closed.

Recovery

Most patients stand with a walker or crutches on the day of or within a few days after partial knee replacement, and physical therapy begins promptly. Return to daily activities may be faster than after total replacement, but timing varies by patient and procedure.

Possible risks

  • Infection
  • Blood clot in the leg or lung
  • Progression of osteoarthritis in other parts of the knee and possible future surgery (total replacement)
  • Implant loosening, wear, or displacement
  • Persistent pain
  • Stiffness and nerve or blood vessel injury (rare)

These risks are managed through appropriate patient selection, preoperative preparation, careful technique, and regular follow-up. Your individual risks will be discussed during your assessment.

When should you see an orthopaedic specialist?

  • Pain focused on the inner or outer side of the knee that has not responded to non-surgical treatment
  • Knee pain that limits walking and climbing stairs
  • After partial replacement: recurrent pain, swelling, or a feeling that the knee is giving way

Frequently asked questions

Who may be a candidate for partial knee replacement?

It may be considered for selected patients whose osteoarthritis is limited to one compartment, whose cruciate ligaments are intact and knee is sufficiently stable, and who have not benefited from non-surgical treatment. Suitability is determined by examination and imaging.

Is partial knee replacement better than total knee replacement?

The two procedures suit different patient groups. In an appropriate candidate, partial replacement may preserve more tissue and allow faster recovery; total replacement is suitable for a broader group and has well-established long-term outcomes. The best option is individual.

How long does recovery take after partial knee replacement?

Most patients stand within the first few days after surgery. Return to daily activities and full recovery vary by patient and procedure.

Could total knee replacement be needed after partial replacement?

Total replacement may be needed if osteoarthritis progresses in other parts of the knee or an implant problem develops. The risk varies by patient and selection.

How long does partial knee replacement last?

Durability depends on the implant, weight, activity level, and surgical technique. It may function for many years in appropriately selected patients; some studies report more frequent revision than with total replacement.

Is partial replacement suitable for younger patients?

It may be considered alongside other options (such as high tibial osteotomy) for selected younger, active patients. Age alone does not determine suitability; the location of arthritis and knee structure matter.
